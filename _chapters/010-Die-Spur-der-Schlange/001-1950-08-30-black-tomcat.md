**Saturday, 30<sup>th</sup> August 1950, 9.50 pm, at the Black Tomcat, 22 Charing Cross Road, London.**

---

Dear Emma,

I am not entirely sure how one is supposed to begin this sort of thing. I am sitting on a draughty windowsill in a room that smells persistently of ancient wood and dusty fabric – rather like Grandmother’s wardrobe, only with a terribly overpowering stench of cedarwood, which has evidently been left out as moth repellent. A thunderstorm is raging outside. The thunder makes the windowpanes rattle in their frames, and the unpredictable crashing is throwing my already unsettled thoughts into total disarray. You would absolutely love it. It is loud, chaotic, and nothing here obeys the laws of physics as we know them.

I was instructed this morning, with a frankly nauseating optimism, to write down everything that happens. Supposedly, this will help me to sort out my head. As if a bit of ink on paper could magically conjure a tidy filing system within one’s brain. But I shall give it a go and write. And since you are the only person I have ever wanted to tell anything to, I am addressing these pages to you.

You have missed so much. I must tell you what happened over the summer. About Voodoo, the scrawny black stray cat I secretly fed for weeks. And about the letter that suddenly changed everything. And above all, about that ghastly holiday.

Shortly after the start of the school holidays, my parents thought it would be a glorious idea to go away. They could think of nothing worse than dragging us off to the seaside, of all places. I sat in the back of our car in the stifling heat, wondering incessantly whether it was cruel intention or merely their boundless ignorance. The sea, Emma. The water, of all things. Just the mere thought of those endless, dark waves made my throat constrict. But to my parents, it was the perfect measure to „take our minds off things“.

And so I sat in the sand for two interminable weeks, the ceaseless roaring of the surf droning in my ears. With every retreat, with every hissing crash against the shore, it seemed to mock me. Whilst the others went bathing or dozed carelessly in the sun, I crouched as far away from the waterline as the beach would allow. With my pale skin and red hair, I possess the evolutionary resilience of a nocturnal cave-dweller anyway. Had I exposed myself to that glaring sun for even five minutes without protection, I should probably have burst spontaneously into flames right before the eyes of the other holidaymakers. So I tucked myself away deep beneath one of the striped parasols into the saving shade. I armed myself with a thick book, pulled my knees to my chest, stared at the printed letters, and simply tried to endure the droning, the shimmering heat, and the intrusive cheerfulness of the other bathers. Thomas was the only one who popped over now and then, dropping down briefly beside me in the shade. That was halfway tolerable, though he, too, was usually far too preoccupied with his own theatricals for the young ladies to be truly present.

Otherwise, my family excelled at their usual, poisonous little routine. Mother and Father bickered incessantly. If they were quiet, it was only because Mother had already sought refuge in her sherry by early afternoon, her eyes taking on that familiar, glassy glint. Margaret strutted up and down the beach like a predator on the prowl for marriageable prey. And Thomas threw his best smile at every girl on the promenade who didn't look away fast enough.

When Thomas wasn’t busy charming young ladies, he and Father would put their heads together to pontificate endlessly about sport, banking, and the economy. Mother and Margaret would reliably use these very moments for their shared „man-watching“. With sharp glances and quiet, calculating remarks, they ruthlessly assessed every passing young fellow on his potential bank balance and his social suitability as a future son-in-law. Throughout all this, I was de facto invisible.

Naturally, not every single second was a punishment. The early mornings were sometimes almost bearable, provided I got up early enough. Whilst Mother slept off her stupor and Father sat indoors at the breakfast table with Margaret and Thomas, the stretch of beach in front of the hotel belonged to me for an hour or two. The air was still crisp then and didn't yet smell of frying fat or the heavy perfume of the promenade crowds, but simply of cool wind and damp sand.

During these quiet hours, I hunted for fossils. I sat in the sand with my head bowed, examining the stones with my magnifying glass and cross-referencing them with my fossil book. That was the only thing on this holiday that was actually any fun. And I genuinely found ammonites! Tiny, perfect spirals baked firmly into the hard limestone. And belemnites, too. They are the fossilised remains of prehistoric squids that feel as smooth and pointed as little projectiles. And on one particularly quiet morning, amongst the fine pebbles, I even discovered minuscule fragments of crinoids. Those are the fossilised remains of ancient sea lilies. I didn't find any intact specimens, mind you, only fragments of their stems. They look like small, precisely grooved cylinders, rather like stacked stone cogwheels. And when these stems break apart into their individual segments, tiny, perfectly formed stars are left behind. Every minuscule piece was precisely preserved down to the very last delicate vein, frozen forever in time.

I have the feeling that whenever I hold such a stone in my hand and run my thumb over the rough, cool surface, the constant noise in my head grows quieter. My pulse settles, and the pressure on my chest eases for a moment. It is truly fascinating. One is holding millions of years of geological history in one’s hand. An organism that has outlasted time itself, simply because it turned to stone under the right pressure. People are loud, unpredictable, and constantly demanding things of me that I do not understand. But a fossil is simple. It doesn’t lie, it doesn’t judge me, it is simply there.

I wrapped several particularly fine specimens in tissue paper and smuggled them into my suitcase. I wish so desperately that I could show them to you. You would probably have laughed at first, because at first glance they look like ordinary, dull stones. But I would have forced you to look through my magnifying glass and explained every single delicate groove to you in minute detail, until you understood the magic behind it just as well as I do.

Sadly, this morning peace never lasted long. Eventually, the rest of the family would wake up, and that would be the end of the quiet. The moment Father decided we had to show our faces to the other people in the hotel, the holiday would turn into a nightmare once more. There was only one single exception during those fourteen days. One single moment when we genuinely felt like a normal family.

That was on a Tuesday afternoon. A frightful thunderstorm rolled over the coast, and the rain lashed against the windows so fiercely that a stroll along the promenade was quite out of the question. Because we couldn't go out, all that pressure to present ourselves perfectly for other people suddenly vanished.

We were all sitting together in the small sitting area of our hotel room. Mother hadn't had any sherry yet, and Father didn't have any important people whose hands he had to shake. Instead, he fetched a pack of cards from his suitcase. We simply sat on the rug and played cards. I am good at remembering patterns and numbers, so I beat Thomas three times in a row. Father laughed – a proper, genuine laugh, not that stiff, put-on laughter he uses for his business acquaintances. To mark the occasion, Margaret didn't even look at me condescendingly. And Mother... Mother didn't heave an exasperated sigh even once just because I had to sort my playing cards meticulously by suit and number before making my move. She simply let me get on with it. Her eyes were perfectly clear, and she didn't have that hard, severe line around her mouth. She even smiled a little when I defeated Thomas – and a real smile, at that, not one of her staged ones for an audience. She simply looked at me like her daughter.

And it was noisy in the room, because Thomas was making such a dramatic fuss about losing, but it was a good noise. A warm noise. For that one afternoon, I had the feeling we might perhaps be a perfectly normal family after all. But it didn't last. As soon as the rain stopped, Father smoothed down his suit again, Mother treated herself to her first glass, and everything was exactly as it had been before.

And then came the evenings. They were an even worse punishment than the afternoons on the beach. Every day, we were squeezed into stiff, scratchy dresses. The fabric chafed against my skin like sand with every step as we strolled along the promenade. For Margaret, the whole thing was a stage. For Father, the perfect opportunity to shake important hands. Thomas copied him eagerly in his immaculate suit and grandly called this superficial banter with business acquaintances „forging valuable connections“. Mother only needed the walks anyway to locate the next establishment serving spirits. Not once in those fourteen days did they partake in anything that went beyond mere trivialities. It was one single, shimmering agony. Too many people pushed past us, packed tightly together. The glaring light of the carousels pierced my eyes, and the aggressive smell of fried fish and roasted almonds coated my tongue like a thick, sticky film. It was so glaringly overwhelming that my own pulse throbbed hard in my throat.

The crowning glory of this theatrical performance was a dinner in our hotel's restaurant. Mother had long since crossed the boundary between „social drinking“ and „silent stupor“, yet she held her back rigidly straight. She fixed me across the silver cutlery with that hostile, ice-cold glare of hers. Her mouth twisted into a sweet smile intended exclusively for the ladies and gentlemen at the neighbouring table, whilst she hissed asking why I couldn't for once be a normal, obedient girl. A girl who dines properly, instead of dissecting her meal with peculiar pedantry. She does not understand that peas and potatoes must not touch on the plate, because every item of food must be tasted individually. If everything mixes into a chaotic mush, my body physically revolts against it and I find it impossible to swallow. Margaret giggled with relish into her napkin. Father absorbed himself in the wine list with sudden, passionate fascination. Thomas, of course, noticed absolutely nothing of this exceedingly cordial atmosphere at our table – he was standing some distance away at the bar, winking at the young cloakroom attendant.

The absolute lowest point, however, was the afternoon on the pier. Mother desperately insisted upon a family photograph for Father's desk at the office. The printed proof of our flawless façade. She dragged us right to the very edge of that rotting wooden structure, directly above the raging surf. Beneath me, the sea crashed through the pillars. With every breaking wave the wood vibrated, and these tremors crept through the soles of my shoes deep into my legs. My throat constricted, as though someone had slipped an invisible rope around my neck and slowly pulled it tight.

But instead of taking me away, Mother's fingers closed around my upper arm like iron claws. She pinched so hard that a sharp pain shot through my shoulder, and muttered through her frozen photographic smile that I had better pull myself together and stop embarrassing them all.

So I stayed put. Rigid. Trapped between my mother’s painful grip and the roaring water beneath me, whilst the photographer took what felt like hours to focus his ridiculous lens.

I do not even remember all the details anymore. All I know is that the sun burned hot on the back of my neck and the rotting wood beneath my feet vibrated with every crash of the waves. The roaring of the sea was so loud that it swallowed up everything else.

And then, quite suddenly, I could not breathe. I tried to draw breath, opened my mouth wide, but nothing arrived. Mother's fingers were still clawing into my arm. I could hear her exasperated words scratching at me – something like: „Stop this theatrical nonsense at once“ – but her voice sounded muffled and strangely far away.

I tried to breathe in. Again and again. No matter how hard I strained and expanded my chest, my throat felt as though it had been tied shut. Tears sprang to my eyes and burned on my sun-scorched cheeks. The sea roared beneath me, and I opened my mouth wide, but my lungs remained empty, as if someone had suddenly created a vacuum all around me. It felt as though an invisible vice was mercilessly crushing my ribcage together, until I simply suffocated there on the wooden planks.

Outlines blurred in the tears, my field of vision grew smaller and smaller. The glaring light of the sun narrowed into a thin line, and then the world simply tilted away and everything went dark.

The next thing I recall are white walls. I woke up in a strange, narrow bed. It smelled of disinfectant and linoleum. No more fish, no salty seawater, no heavy perfume. Only disinfectant and the quiet clattering of medical instruments. I was told later that Thomas had snatched me up from the wooden planks and carried me off the pier. He had apparently run straight towards the emergency doctors with me in his arms. At least one person in this family possesses rudimentary reflexes in an emergency. For Mother and Father, of course, this place was merely an embarrassing blemish on the canvas of their flawless holiday. For me, it was the most peaceful afternoon of the entire fortnight.

A doctor eventually explained to my parents what had happened. Asthma. It is a spasmodic constriction of the airways in response to stimuli or stress. They gave me an inhaler. A rather cumbersome little contraption containing a fine, dry powder. I have to press it deep into my throat and inhale sharply, so that the powder forces the blocked airways to open up again.

Ever since that day on the pier, I have carried this piece of plastic with me constantly. To Mother, it is medically certified proof that I am not only unbearable with my eccentricities, but that I now also constitute a physical burden. She remains firmly convinced that my shortness of breath is pure „theatricality“, with which I deliberately embarrass her in public.

To me, it is a tool. When the world becomes too loud, when people press too closely to me, or when the walls close in menacingly during my nights, I cling to this plastic. It affords me, at least, the illusion of control. A breath of powder, a chemical reaction, and the lungs function once more. If only the rest of my life were quite so predictable.

When we were finally back in Harrow, I scarcely spent any time in the house. My room had become nothing more than a place to sleep. The only thing tying me to this house was Voodoo. Do you remember the black stray tomcat who always prowled through our neighbourhood with that confident, slightly dangerous look? The one Mrs Gable from next door always frantically hurried her trembling poodles away from? That is him. I have been secretly feeding him for months, with leftovers from supper that I wrapped in my handkerchief. Mother would likely faint. Black cat hairs upon her floral upholstery? An absolute scandal. Besides, according to her highly scientific opinion, black cats bring nothing but bad luck. But he belongs to me now. You would love him, too. He is not afraid of anything. And he does not speak, he does not judge, and above all, he does not force me to smile. Quite unlike my parents.

The only places where I could properly breathe at all these past few weeks smelled of old paper, dried ink, and floor wax: the municipal library and the Natural History Museum in London.

The contrast was enormous. At home, I was a constant disappointment, a defective cog in the family machinery. But at the library, I was... right. Mrs Higgins, a plump lady who always smelled comfortingly of peppermint, and Mr Abernathy with his perpetually ink-stained fingers soon knew me so well that they set aside the latest scientific journals for me. They even shared their sandwiches with me during their breaks. We would sit in the tiny back room over tea, and they would speak to me. Seriously, and as an equal. Nobody put on that high, artificial melody that adults so often use for children, and nobody dismissed my precise way of speaking as „peculiar“. Mrs Higgins had somehow understood that glaring light gives me a sharp pain behind the eyes. Therefore, only a small, dim lamp was ever lit at my reading spot. On some days, I was permitted to stay after official closing time. When the iron lock on the front door clicked into place and I sat alone amongst the silent, ordered rows of shelves, I finally felt understood. Books do not demand eye contact.

And then there was the museum. Because I was there almost every day, incessantly making sketches and notes, the staff eventually knew me by name. Especially Dr Hastings, the elderly curator with his neatly trimmed moustache and the small magnifying glass on a silver chain. He spoke seldom. And when he did, it was exclusively about facts and exhibits. He never demanded that I talk about myself or exchange polite pleasantries. I was allowed to stand beside him, listen, and simply exist. When I asked questions, he explained the answers to me patiently and with understanding. At home I was frequently scolded for my curiosity and impatiently sent away, yet here my thirst for knowledge was not considered an exhausting nuisance.

Because he noticed that we shared this quiet fascination, he took me behind the scenes one day without any grand explanations. Into the hidden world of museum work. It smelled sharply of chemicals, damp plaster, and old wood. He showed me the departments where men in white coats, far away from the noisy crowds of visitors, crouched with intense concentration over delicate wire frames. Dr Hastings explained to me in a hushed voice how they prepared the animals. I watched as tiny glass eyes were carefully inserted and feathers or strands of fur were nudged into position, a hair's breadth at a time with fine tweezers, to create a perfect illusion. Next door, staff were bent over large drawing boards, designing perfectly scaled miniature models for upcoming exhibitions. I was allowed to peer into heavy, wooden archive boxes and saw how meticulously every single minute discovery was documented. It was a world of ordered science. A place where every detail, no matter how small, found attention and respect. It was magnificent.

And yet... when I later wandered alone through the large, echoing exhibition halls, I often stood before the glass cases for hours on end. I felt close to all these taxidermied animals in a strange, sad sort of way. One might think that behind the thick glass, they were finally safe. That, however, is a fallacy. Every day, loud, inconsiderate crowds of people stream past them. They gawk at them, point at them with greasy fingers, or tap dully against the panes. The animals are ruthlessly subjected to this constant staring. They cannot defend themselves, cannot turn away, or even so much as close their eyes.

The longer I observed them, the more my throat tightened. I had seen in the workshop how they were made, after all. With cold wires and sharp pliers, a posture had been forced upon them that was not their own at all. They had simply been bent into the shape that most pleased the people in front of the glass – a bird with eternally, majestically outspread wings; a little fox in a perpetually elegant mid-leap. They had been forced into a flawless, pleasing image, solely so that they looked appealing to others.

I believe that is why the sight made me feel so sorry for them. They were rigidly frozen in an artificial form determined by others, without ever being permitted to be themselves again. It was exactly like the evenings on the promenade, when I was squeezed into a stiff dress and fiercely instructed to kindly present a „normal“ picture. With the cruel difference that these animals have to endure their corset of wire for all eternity.

My routine then shattered on a late Thursday afternoon, somewhere between my visits to the library and the museum.

The very moment I stepped into the hallway, I felt an icy chill in the air that had nothing to do with the temperature. Sitting in the sitting room was a strange woman who did not fit into the meticulous, sterile perfection of our house in the slightest. She gave me a smiling wink, and the first thing that practically leapt to my eye was a sartorial scandal: she was wearing trousers. Wide, high-waisted tailored trousers in a striking black and mustard-yellow check pattern. For a woman, that was already an unusual, daring sight anyway, but here, upon Mother’s floral armchairs, it bordered on an open declaration of war. With this, she wore a light-coloured blouse with short puffed sleeves, a black bow at the collar, and over it a black, perfectly fitted waistcoat with silver buckles. Her wild, auburn curls were only just managed by a few hairpins.

She had soft, laughing creases around her eyes and smelled incredibly comforting. Not of perfume, but of sun-dried wood radiating the heat of the day, mingled with a pleasantly tart pipe tobacco. With a warm Irish accent, in which she softly rolled her R’s, she introduced herself as Professor Niamh O’Callaghan – a teacher from a school by the name of Hogwarts.

Mother and Father were visibly displeased at having to entertain the Professor until I finally came home. Their lips were pressed into thin lines, and Father was pointedly staring at his pocket watch. When Professor O’Callaghan then handed me a heavy letter and explained what I was – a witch – and where I was to go – to this Hogwarts boarding school – I was initially utterly bewildered. Firstly, I was supposed to be starting at grammar school after the holidays, and secondly: witches do not exist. That is fairy-tale nonsense. But the thick paper in my hands felt exceedingly real. I had already memorised the exact bus timetable and knew precisely where I was going to sit! And now I was supposed to go to a boarding school for magic in the far-off Scottish Highlands? My head was spinning, and my tummy fluttered peculiarly. Everything was suddenly different. But this strange Professor smiled at me so warmly and kindly, as if it weren't strange at all, but rather the most normal thing in the world. Thomas was standing slightly apart, and when our eyes met for a fraction of a second, I saw that he was happy for me. Yet before I could even ask my first question, chaos erupted.

The sharp, sickly-sweet stench of sherry wafted towards me from Mother again. It was as if her forced mask had shattered to the floor. She crossed her arms and stared at me. In her eyes lay that pure, unfathomable hatred I knew so well. An icy pang that put a stone in my stomach every single time. But deep beneath it, for a split second, something else flickered that I could not decipher. She hissed, cold as ice, that she had always known it – I was a „changeling“. Margaret, leaning in the doorway, instantly parroted it with a spiteful snigger. Father simply sat in his armchair, playing the silent, decorative piece of furniture as usual, as if none of this concerned him.

The heat rose to the back of my neck. My face burned. It was dreadfully humiliating that my family was treating me so degradingly in front of this nice woman. I should have liked nothing more than for the ground to swallow me whole. But if I am entirely honest with myself, Emma, deep down I had expected nothing else. When had they ever given me the feeling they loved me? To them, I had always been nothing but an embarrassing problem that needed to be hidden away. My pulse raced nonetheless, and I hastily tried to string together some suitable words to stop the quarrel.

But Professor O’Callaghan was cut from a different cloth. She cut Mother off in mid-sentence. With a sharpness in her accent that brooked no argument, she gave her such a dressing-down that my own breath caught. She called that derogatory way of speaking about one's own child an absolute disgrace. She made it unequivocally clear that such narrow-mindedness and blind hatred were the most repulsive things she had ever encountered. She put Mother firmly in her place, as if she were a malicious, naughty schoolchild.

My heart was in my mouth. Never in my life had anyone spoken to Mother like that. Least of all on my behalf. It terrified me, because I knew that Father and Mother would take it out on me the moment the front door clicked shut, and yet, at the same time, it felt so incredibly good. But this also jolted Father out of his apathy. His pride was wounded. He stood up abruptly, drew himself up to his full height, and instructed the Professor in a cutting voice to leave our house at once. He would not tolerate such disrespect under his own roof.

Professor O’Callaghan drew herself up to her full height. She turned towards the door but paused on the threshold. With an almost frightening, icy calm, she pointed out to my parents that, despite this performance, it was their duty to accompany me to a place in London called Diagon Alley to purchase school supplies. And the following day, they would have to take me to the railway station so I could catch the train.

At that, Mother finally lost her temper entirely. She refused point-blank. A shopping trip for a school of magic did not fit into her diary. Father interjected, half placatingly, half condescendingly, that they had no time for such nonsense. They had preparations to make for a stay of several days at an exclusive country estate – together with his employer and his family. Margaret immediately brushed a strand of hair from her face coquettishly. She had evidently long since calculated the employer's son as her future husband.

The Professor retorted sharply that the elementary education of one's own child was surely just as important as shaking hands at a country estate. A heated exchange of words ensued. Father insisted upon his social obligations, whilst Professor O'Callaghan argued in disbelief that this was my entrance into a new world and I needed someone by my side. But she was met with a brick wall of snobbish disinterest.

In the end, she merely shook her head. She made no secret of her disappointment. She left no doubt that she utterly despised such a degree of glaring neglect.

After these damning words, Professor O’Callaghan turned away, leaving my parents standing in the sitting room, speechless with indignation. I watched her go, and suddenly something inside my chest tightened painfully. It was an oppressive, hot sensation just behind my ribs. A thick lump formed in my throat, making it hard to swallow. For the very first time in my life, an adult had stood up to protect me. Someone had defended me against my family. I stared at the open door, and everything in me cried out for her to stay.

I ran out after her as far as the verandah. The wood of the floorboards was still warm from the afternoon sun. When she turned around, she did not look down at me from above. She crouched down until our eyes were level and placed her hands on my shoulders. Once again, that comforting smell of dry wood and tobacco enveloped me. Her gaze was soft, but without that oppressive, uncomfortable pity that adults so often display when they speak to me.

In her gentle, Irish voice, she spoke to me quietly. She said I was exactly right, just the way I was. That the people in that house were simply blind to my worth, and that this was entirely their own failing, not mine. She called me clever. She said I was special and would belong with them from this day forward. A fierce pressure built up behind my eyes, and my vision blurred. I clamped my teeth together and forced the tears back into my body through sheer willpower.

Then someone yanked the front door open. Father and Mother stood in the frame, and the volume simply exploded. Father bellowed that he would telephone for the police on the spot if this person did not leave his property that very instant. The aggressive wave of noise made me flinch instinctively, but the Professor did not bat an eyelid. She did not turn her head in their direction. She held my gaze steadily and carried on speaking, entirely undisturbed.

Shaking her head, she pulled a small flyer from the roomy pocket of her checked trousers and pressed it into my hand. It was an advertisement for an escort service, issued by an establishment called „the Black Tomcat“. She explained that she would be only too glad to accompany me herself, but unfortunately, it was not possible for her. She would not even be my teacher at Hogwarts, as she was on the verge of leaving for the United States to teach at another school there. And the rest of the teaching staff had already been assigned to other pupils from non-magical families. So I would have to make this journey alone.

Then she threw Mother and Father a very quiet, calculating sidelong glance. She instructed me not to worry about the payment. If my family refused to finance the escort service, they should simply send the bill from „the Black Tomcat“ directly to her. She added that the owls would know where they needed to go. I am not entirely sure what nocturnal birds of prey are supposed to do with invoice amounts. But she sounded terribly serious.

That was the final straw. Father lost the very last of his composure. The fact that someone was offering him financial charity under his own roof amounted to a declaration of war on his pride. He bellowed that nobody in this family would accept acts of charity; he would, of course, settle this bill out of his own pocket. In two furious strides, he was beside us and roughly snatched the paper from my fingers.

Professor O’Callaghan rose slowly. She squeezed my shoulders firmly one last time and wished me all the very best. Then she turned away, leaving me on the verandah with the letter and a shopping list, whilst Father clutched the crumpled piece of paper in his fist.

It was a ghastly feeling. Because of Father's loud bellowing, everything had ended up happening far too quickly. I hadn't had the chance to ask a single one of my questions. I know absolutely nothing about this school! How does magic work? What happens if I can't do it at all? What on earth am I supposed to pack? And what is expected of me now? My stomach tied itself into tight knots, like a tangled rope. Yesterday, I knew exactly how my life was going to proceed, and now, suddenly, I am a witch and have to travel to Scotland all by myself. I stared after the Professor from the verandah, while the evening breeze brushed coolly against my damp cheeks. At the corner of the street, she paused, turned around one last time, and raised her hand in an encouraging wave. Then she stepped into the red telephone box at the junction. Once again, that burning pressure rose behind my eyes; I blinked for a mere fraction of a second to clear the moisture – and when I opened my eyelids again, the box was empty. She was gone as quickly as she had arrived.

With burning eyes and a thick lump in my throat, I turned around and went back into the house. Father had long since vanished, doubtless into his study. Margaret and Thomas had likewise vanished off the face of the earth. Only Mother was still standing in the dark hallway, the half-empty glass of sherry in her hand, staring at me. Her look was so icy that it gave me goosepimples.

I asked her quietly if that had just been real, or if my brain was playing tricks on me. Mother merely let out a contemptuous snort and replied coldly that she earnestly wished it weren't so. With an exaggerated, mocking flick of her wrist, she raised her glass, congratulated me on my oh-so-marvellous future, downed the remaining drop, and left me standing in the hallway. My stomach cramped painfully. Not even now, when my entire life was being turned upside down and I would finally be out of her way, could she be nice to me. And to think I had always believed that was exactly what she wanted.

I just wanted to get to my room, lock the door from the inside, and have my saving silence. Yet when I opened the door, the little desk lamp was already on, and Thomas was sitting on the edge of my bed. He was still wearing his good waistcoat, but he had loosened his tie, which, for him, the eternal model son, looked almost rebellious. As I entered, he silently patted the spot next to him.

Hesitantly, I sat down beside him. For a moment, we simply sat next to one another and stared at the rug. The ticking of the grandfather clock drifted up to us from the hallway. Every chime felt like a little hammer against my temples.

Thomas looked at me from the side. His face was soft and worried. He said quietly that he had noticed how difficult this summer had been for me. That I was even quieter than usual and looked as though I were carrying far too heavy a burden around with me. He asked if I would like to talk about it.

My heart skipped a beat. He knew nothing. Even if a few scraps of information were known in this house, nobody here has the faintest inkling of who you truly are to me. Nobody grasps how infinitely important our own little world is to me. How could I possibly explain that to Thomas? I would never expose this sacred secret of ours to the judgemental glares of this family. My throat felt tight. I merely shook my head and hastily wiped away a tear that had escaped down my cheek. Thomas nodded slowly. He didn't pry. I shall always credit him for that.

Finally, he broke the silence again. It sounded unexpectedly harsh when he snorted and called our family a bunch of elitist, narrow-minded ignoramuses and heartless hypocrites. The lot of them. He confessed quietly how terribly sorry he was that he had so rarely been there for me these past few years. He admitted frankly that he had simply looked the other way whenever they punished me again for my ways. Because it was much easier for him to play the comfortable role of the perfect son rather than cross them.

But he swore to me that he had always known I was different – and not defective, mind you, but special. He was firmly convinced that I was destined for greater things than rotting away here in this stifling suburb, stuck in a family that hadn't the foggiest idea what a brilliant person they actually had before them. The tears I had so painstakingly held back until now burned fiercely beneath my eyelids.

He made a vague gesture across the room that encompassed the entire house, and assured me that all of this was not forever. Once I came of age, I should never have to return here again if I didn't wish to. He told me I could then build a life of my own, by my own rules. He was absolutely certain I would find my people – people who appreciated my peculiarities, who loved that I saw things so clearly and in such a structured way, and who would value and love me exactly as I am. Sometimes, he added quietly, one simply has to search a little longer to find them.

He edged a fraction closer. Hesitantly, he raised his hand, but let it hover in the air next to my shoulder. He knows I do not like embraces, that they feel as though I am being caged in. He whispered, with a sad glint in his eye, that he should like awfully to give me a tight hug right now. Instead, he let his hand drop and merely patted the mattress beside me gently twice. That was better than any embrace. Then he stood up and gently advised me to sort out my thoughts. After all, we both knew I still had a fair bit to teach this new world out there. At that sentence, despite everything, I couldn't help but smile a tiny bit.

He went quietly to the door, cast me one last warm look, and closed it carefully behind him. As the click of the lock echoed in the room and I was finally alone, the rope that had so painstakingly held me together all evening snapped.

The moment the silence enveloped me completely, everything came crashing down on me. I had begun the day so peacefully – first the scent of old paper in the library, then the orderly world of the museum. I had been almost content when I came home. And then suddenly there was this strange woman in her checked trousers, and everything I had built up in the way of security had crumbled in seconds.

I simply let myself fall backwards onto the bed, pulled my knees to my chest so tightly it hurt, and buried my face in my arms. The way Mother had looked at me during the conversation – as if I were a piece of vermin one would prefer to sweep straight off the rug – cut deeper than any word. Her contempt was so quiet, yet so terribly poisonous. And Father... he had used this whole situation once again as a gorilla-like display of power. The bellowing, the insisting upon his house, his pride, his rules – it was never about me, but only about showing this strange woman who was master of the house. It was so predictable, and yet it had caught me completely off guard once again.

The fact that Thomas had sat down beside me, that he had seen and named all of this – that shook me more than the row with Mother. It was the first time anyone from my own family had shown me that my world isn't wrong. That I am not invisible and defective simply because I do not fit into their narrow pigeonholes. But that only made it all the harder. If only he knew how suffocating it feels when the only person who truly understands one is suddenly so far away... he hasn't a clue that my heart was already broken before his visit.

I tried the entire evening to stay strong. I wanted to feel like one of those pebbles I collected on the beach – smooth, hard, untouchable. But I am not a pebble. I am just Ella. And I am so infinitely tired of this constant battle.

I wept until my eyes burned and my head felt as though it would split open. I cried it all out: the fear of this new world, the anger at my parents' narrow-mindedness, and this dreadful loneliness that eats away at me a little more every day. And the next morning, I had to function again. I had to pretend once more to be the quiet, compliant Ella. But on that evening, it was simply no longer possible.

The next morning, I simply crept back into my old routine. The weeks that followed I spent almost exclusively in the library and the museum. I wanted to find real, logical facts about this school. But in the great atlases and encyclopaedias, there was no place named „Hogwarts“. When I looked for witches or wizards, I found only made-up stories: Shakespeare's Macbeth, silly fairy tales about enchanted spinning wheels, or old accounts of witch burnings at Pendle Hill. None of this offered me any solid preparation. I pored over books on folklore for hours on end. The only thing amongst all these illogical fairy tales that felt even slightly like real power were accounts of West African religions and Haitian rituals. That is why I named the tomcat Voodoo. Mother would utterly despise the name if she knew what it meant. She would find it vulgar and „heathen“. Good.

Thomas could not help me with my investigations either. He left for university in Birmingham a few days earlier than planned. For him, the flawless model son, there was, naturally, a celebratory farewell in the sitting room before he departed. Father gave a pompous speech to his future heir, Mother theatrically dabbed a few tears from her face with a lace handkerchief – the sherry glass ever at the ready in her other hand – whilst delicate little sandwiches were served. It was a perfectly staged little show, during which I, as always, merely stood silently on the sidelines.

That brings me to today, the 30<sup>th</sup> of August 1950.

When I woke up, my head felt as though it had been split in two. On the one hand, fear of the unknown tied my throat so tightly I could scarcely swallow. But at the same time, my entire body tingled with relief, because I knew: today, I am finally allowed to escape this house.

Breakfast in the dining room was glacial. Mother didn't even look at me; she merely pushed my plate towards me in silence across the polished wood. Father had entrenched himself behind his newspaper and was grumbling loudly about the prices on the stock exchange. Margaret sat at the other end of the table, boredly twirling a strand of hair around her finger. Not a single soul wasted a word of farewell on me. It was as though I had already moved out.

The only interruption was the postman, who brought me a small parcel. Inside was this beautiful book I am currently writing in. It is a parting gift from Thomas. He must have specifically arranged the parcel before his departure so that it would arrive punctually today as a surprise. It is bound in thick, brown leather, which looks rather dark and worn at the edges, as if it has already lived a history of its own. A crest is embossed deeply into the leather on the cover: a large „H“ in the middle, surrounded by a lion, a serpent, a badger, and an eagle. Beneath it is a motto in Latin: „Draco dormiens nunquam titillandus“. The book can be fastened with a sturdy leather strap and a tarnished brass buckle. It looks wonderfully old. I love it, Emma.

Between the thick, parchment-like pages lay a little card from him. In it, he wished me a marvellous time and advised me to use this book to sort my experiences, thoughts, and feelings, should the world there become too loud for me. He knows perfectly well how much sorting things helps me. He also wrote that he could simply advise me to be brave now – after all, that had always been easy for him. But that was precisely what he did not ask of me.

He explained that I do not have to force myself to cast off my fears or feign fearlessness. I just mustn't allow the fear to make me invisible and rob me of this chance. Then there were lines that brought me near to tears. He called my mind brilliant and asked me to show the people there, at my very own pace, what he sees in me. At the end, he promised that everything would be alright, and wrote that he loves me.

I had to swallow hard when I read that. He understood that I cannot simply be brave like he is. He does not ask me to pretend or to ignore my uneasy feelings. He gave me permission, so to speak, to be afraid. That made the heavy pressure on my chest a little bit lighter.

After breakfast, I went straight back to my room. I sat on my bed, packed the last few odds and ends into my trunk, pondered which books I wished to take with me, and carefully slipped Thomas's card back between the pages of my book. After that, I repeatedly ran my fingers over its rough leather. It helped to soothe me and kill the sluggish time until my departure.

Eventually, a deafening noise and sheer pandemonium broke out in the rest of the house. Everyone was frantically busy packing enormous steamer trunks and gathering their gear. For today was their big day, too: the all-important and all-decisive meeting with Father's bank manager was upon them. The one for which they could not accompany me. They are all travelling to an exclusive country estate, to some elitist country club. Five days that will consist of nothing but playing golf, hunting, endless handshaking, and that dreadful, false laughter. And naturally, this is the perfect stage for Mother and Father to auction Margaret off to the manager's son, or some similarly suitable candidate. I already feel genuinely sorry for the poor chap who ultimately gets chosen for my sister.

As I listened to the commotion from my room, I suddenly realised something. Somehow, it seems I have had a blessing in disguise. On the one hand, my throat tightened at the thought of having to travel to London entirely on my own and figure everything out for myself. My heart beat much too fast just thinking about it. But had that letter not arrived, I should have had to go to that country estate with them. I should have had to smile mutely in scratchy dresses for five days, shake hands with Father’s loud business acquaintances, and endure Margaret’s vain giggling. The mere thought of it makes my stomach knot up unpleasantly. I much preferred to travel alone to this „Black Tomcat“, whatever might await me there. And then, the time had come. My watch read half past four. I took a deep breath, grabbed my trunk, and went downstairs.

On the small sideboard next to the front door lay a piece of paper. It was a cheque for the escort service from „the Black Tomcat“. Mother and Father preferred to pay these strangers, simply so that I should be quietly and punctually cleared out of the way.

My disappearance thus had an exact price: 35 Galleons. If I have understood the exchange rates on my shopping list correctly, that equates to roughly 3 pounds, 18 shillings, and 7 pence. For Mrs Gable from next door, who has to pinch every penny to redeem her ration books for a little tea or butter, that is an entire week’s wages. For Mother and Father, it was merely a tiresome tip. My value – or rather, the value of my absence – had thereby been precisely quantified. It did not surprise me; it was merely the proof, written in ink, of what I already knew anyway. I took the cheque, opened the door, and stepped out into the pouring rain. The heavy click of the front door behind me was the only thing to accompany my departure. No one called after me.

Only Voodoo was crouching, perfectly dry, beneath the large rhododendron bushes by the garden gate. I stopped and looked at him. Right at the bottom of the Professor's shopping list was a sentence I had memorised exactly: *Students may also bring an owl OR a cat OR a toad.* I wouldn't have left him with Margaret or my parents for all the world. They would have chased him away or worse, the moment they realised I was gone.

I crouched down beside him on the wet stones. He did not hesitate for a second, leapt onto my shoulder with a deft bound, and wrapped himself snugly around my neck like a warm collar, purring loudly. With my heavy trunk in one hand and my open umbrella in the other, I battled my way through the pelting wet to the stop and waited for the omnibus to London.

Since pets are forbidden on the buses, I had to smuggle him aboard unnoticed. Before getting on, I closed the umbrella and hastily pulled my coat as tightly around myself as possible. Voodoo curled up beneath the fabric. The driver did give me a rather puzzled look as I boarded, because my neck appeared unnaturally thick beneath the clothing, but he said nothing.

It was exactly the same route that was familiar to me from our previous school trips to the Natural History Museum. Knowing at which junctions the bus would turn gave me a tiny bit of security amidst this chaos. It smelled constantly of wet wool, damp newsprint, and stale cigarette smoke.

At some point, a man in a greasy overcoat sat down right next to me, even though there were plenty of empty seats. He encroached far too closely upon my personal space. He smelled sourly of alcohol and stale sweat. Voodoo was by now sitting well hidden down in the footwell between my legs. He was evidently just as displeased by the intrusive presence of this stranger. The man had not noticed him yet, however. He stared at me incessantly, offered in a raspy voice to help me pass the time, and repeatedly licked his dry lips as he did so.

My heartbeat quickened instantly and I edged closer to the window. Voodoo let out a quiet hiss. The man leaned in until I could feel his warm, damp breath against my ear. He whispered what a pretty little kitten I had there, and asked if he might stroke it. The fur on the back of Voodoo's neck stood on end, and a deep rumble vibrated in his throat.

I froze. My body felt turned to ice. The man's breathing grew louder, and he slid his hand across the seat cushion a fraction of an inch at a time, ever closer to my leg. He whispered that the driver would surely throw me and the cat straight out onto the rainy street if he knew about my stowaway. But if he were allowed to stroke it just a little, it would remain our secret. He tucked a strand of hair behind my ear. His fingers smelled revoltingly of cigarette smoke. He smiled a repulsive smile and said that I couldn't very well parade about in public with such a lovely kitty and expect that nobody would want to touch it.

Voodoo hissed sharply, bared his teeth, and raised a paw with flashing claws. But I could not move. My head was screaming that I must stand up and run away, yet my body did not respond. It was as if I were encased in a thick block of ice. Everything inside me clenched tight.

At the next stop, an elderly lady in a neat two-piece suit boarded the bus. She saw us, and her eyes narrowed behind her thick spectacles. In a sharp, loud tone that cut through the entire bus, she ordered the man to vacate his seat at once and leave her niece alone. The man flinched. He muttered a few foul words under his breath, stood up hastily, and fled outside. The sour smell slowly vanished with him.

The elderly lady remained standing in the aisle. She smiled at me and asked quietly if everything was alright. The block of ice around me shattered, and suddenly I was trembling all over. I merely nodded, fighting against the tears that burned hotly in my eyes.

She enquired with concern where I was travelling to all alone, and urged me earnestly to beware of such dreadful characters. Then she asked quite politely if she might sit down next to me, so that nothing of the sort should happen again on the remainder of the journey.

The fact that she asked first, and didn't simply encroach upon me uninvited, gave me the feeling that I could properly breathe again. I was so grateful for this careful help and nodded hastily. With her at my side, Voodoo relaxed noticeably too. He scrambled quietly up from the footwell onto my lap and even allowed the lady to stroke him – all of which we did in strict secrecy beneath my drawn-together coat, so the driver wouldn't notice the tomcat and throw us out on our ears.

Through our little, secret conspiracy, we fell into quiet conversation, and I learned a little more about her. She told me her name was Dorothy Blythe and that she worked as a seamstress in a small atelier in Soho. That explained why her suit looked so very neat and every single seam was perfect.

She sat bolt upright in her seat, but as she spoke about herself, her voice suddenly sounded heavy and tired. The war had taken almost everything from her. She told me that she had lost her husband during the evacuation of Dunkirk in 1940. Only a few months later, her only daughter had perished in the air raids on London. She didn't cry when she said it, but her eyes looked as though they had already shed enough tears in the past to last a lifetime. My stomach instantly tied itself in knots. I did not know what the correct response to something so awful was.

But Mrs Blythe didn't seem angry at the world at all. She noticed immediately that my hands were still trembling slightly because of that repulsive man, and that I didn't know where to look. So she changed the subject. She peered under my coat and praised Voodoo. She called him a „brave little panther“. Then she looked at me, smiled, and said that a clever girl travelling alone with such a fierce protector needn't be afraid of anyone in the world. Her kind words were like a warm plaster. We talked a little longer until I had to alight at my stop and change to the next bus.

For the remainder of the journey, I was alone once more. Outside, the wet London streets drifted past the fogged-up windows, and inside my head, things grew dreadfully loud. My tummy fluttered with nerves when I thought of this school of magic, yet at the same time, a thick, heavy stone lay in my chest. I thought of you the entire time. Every mile away from home meant not just leaving my old life behind. Which, considering the familial situation, is probably no bad thing; but above all, it meant that the geographical distance between us was growing ever wider, and with every stop I was moving further away from you. Every step into this new world felt as though I were leaving you behind, and would from now on be entirely on my own. I wish so desperately that you could sit here beside me, and that we could do all this together.

Finally, I arrived. I lugged my heavy trunk across the wet pavement. Voodoo clung to my neck, purring loudly, and my umbrella kept us as dry as it could. I scanned the street signs until I found the district that hadn't been destroyed by the war. And after a while, I stood before the correct house number. Before „the Black Tomcat“.

By now it was coming down in buckets, and the wind tore roughly at my wet coat. The building looked as though it might topple over at any moment. It was lopsided and squat. By all the laws of physics, it ought to have collapsed in on itself long ago, but somehow it held itself upright. The heavy, dark wooden door stood ajar. I set my trunk down on the slippery stones, took one last deep breath, and peered hesitantly into the dark interior.

You would have loved it, Emma. This building was no ordinary hotel. The first thing that caught my eye were the animals. Cats lay upon every tiny available patch of space. Voodoo crept out from under my wet coat immediately. He walked confidently into the warm parlour, briefly sniffed at a few of the other cats, and then lay down directly in front of the crackling fire, as though he had lived here all his life.

Small, rustic tables and chairs stood haphazardly about the room, as if someone had simply dropped them at random. In a sitting area next to the entrance, a boy was fast asleep on a worn sofa. The roaring storm outside did not bother him in the slightest. Everything in this room was covered in a fine layer of dust and cat hair. It smelled pungent, but good in a comforting sort of way – a dense mixture of old wood, smoke, and wet animal fur.

Behind the bar, an old woman was asleep. Yet that was not what made me freeze and my knees go weak. It was what was happening on the floor: brooms were sweeping there. All by themselves. Without hands. They glided across the floorboards in even, rhythmic sweeps, as if invisible workers were holding them tight. I stood petrified in the doorway and stared at the twitching straw. I looked for wires or a contraption. But there was nothing. It was magic.

I stepped inside and, with great effort, pushed the heavy wooden door shut against the howling wind. The sudden silence in the room, broken only by the crackling of the fire and the delicate scratching of the brooms, was a stark contrast to the storm outside. Cautiously, I pushed my trunk through the labyrinth of dozing cats and dancing brooms until I reached the counter.

I cleared my throat quietly, but the old woman was sleeping so soundly that not even her eyelashes twitched. Then I spotted a small, tarnished brass bell on the wood. Hesitantly, I pressed it and a bright ting cut through the air.

The woman started awake with an inarticulate grunt. And in the exact split second her still entirely cloudy gaze fell upon me, loud, clattering clack, clack, clack noises rang out, making me flinch so violently that I almost dropped my umbrella. The sweeping brooms had suddenly fallen lifeless onto the floorboards. I stared at the unmoving straw. Blinking, the woman brushed a stringy grey curl from her face and asked in a scratchy voice what she could do for me.

My fingers were trembling slightly as I pulled the wet, crumpled flyer from „the Black Tomcat“ out of my pocket. I held it out to her and said quietly that I hoped I was in the right place. My heart was hammering against my ribs. What if this was all a mistake, I was in the wrong part of London, and this entire magical invitation was nothing but a bad joke by Cynthia? The woman merely cast a brief glance at the paper and exhaled noisily. At that very moment, the brooms behind me hovered into the air again. The rhythmic scratching against the wood simply carried on, as if the interruption had never occurred.

The landlady leaned heavily on the counter and gave me a friendly nod. I breathed a sigh of relief. At least I wasn't in the wrong place. I pulled the wet cheque for the 35 Galleons from my pocket, slid it across the rough wood, and tried to sound as grown-up as possible as I explained that I wished to make use of this escort service.

She fetched a thick ledger, bound in heavy leather, from beneath the counter and asked for my name. I gave it to her. She traced the lines several times with a long, bony finger. Then she frowned. I wasn't listed there, she muttered. I wasn't booked in.

At that moment, the wooden floor seemed to drop away beneath my feet. My throat constricted so tightly that I lost my breath. This was yet another piece of ice-cold proof. My parents had tossed this cheque on the sideboard and simply put me out the door. They hadn't even telephoned here. One tiny telephone call to ensure that their eleven-year-old child had a place to sleep in London – I wasn't even worth those two minutes of their precious time. Tears sprang to my eyes instantly, but I bit the inside of my cheek so hard that I had the metallic taste of blood on my tongue. I would never show this humiliation before a stranger. I forced my hands to stop trembling, swallowed hard, and lied. I said Father must surely have simply overlooked it in all the fuss surrounding his important business appointments.

The old woman looked at me in silence for a long moment. That uncomfortable knowingness flickered in her eyes. She saw right through my excuse immediately, I could feel it precisely. But to my relief, she let it drop. She merely snorted quietly, examined the cheque more closely, and said that the paper was, after all, backed by a Gringotts partner bank. And I was in luck, as there was still a bed free in the girls' dormitory.

I was simply to leave my trunk down there. A so-called „house-elf“ would bring it up later. I hadn't the faintest idea what a house-elf was, but going by my observation with the invisibly guided brooms, I strongly suspected it might be invisible.

She rummaged a heavy iron key out of a drawer and placed it with a clatter onto the wood. Only then did I realise how freezing I actually was. My wet hair clung to my face, and water was dripping from my coat onto the floorboards. The old woman looked me up and down. Her gaze suddenly became so compassionate that it almost hurt. She asked if I should like a large cup of hot tea. Because otherwise, I looked as if I was about to freeze solid on her.

I nodded gratefully and asked for peppermint tea, if it wasn't too much trouble. The thought of Mrs Higgins in the library and her peppermint scent was genuinely comforting in that moment. The old woman said it was no bother and vanished, shuffling through a little door into a small room behind the bar.

I left my trunk by the counter and went over to the crackling fireplace. Voodoo had already curled himself up there into a black, purring ball on the rug. I sank onto a chair, pulled my knees to my chest, and tried to stop shivering. My wet clothes were now clinging coldly to my skin, and the damp fabric began to scratch abominably with every movement.

The heat of the fire struggled to penetrate my clothing. Fire is actually a very predictable thing. Or so I had thought until now, at any rate. It requires oxygen, heat, and fuel, and it famously flickers in shades of yellow, orange, and red. I was just about losing myself in the quiet, warm dance of the flames when it suddenly changed.

First, there was a loud hissing and spitting, followed directly by an eerie whooshing. It sounded as though a Hoover were sucking air downwards through the chimney. And then the fire suddenly turned. From one second to the next, the flames became bright green! A proper, glowing, toxic emerald green. They shot wildly upwards, filling the entire fireplace, but funnily enough, not a single hot spark flew out into the room towards me.

Right in the middle of this crazy green fire, a dark shadow suddenly appeared, spinning like a top. There was a loud rumble, as if someone were tripping over the stones in the hearth, someone coughed, and the next moment, a girl stumbled straight out of the fire. Just like that! Right out of the flames. And in the very same split second she stepped out, the enormous green fire simply shrank back down and carried on burning away quite dutifully in yellow and red, as if nothing had happened.

It came so suddenly that it frightened the life out of me. I let out a terribly embarrassing shriek, flailed my arms wildly in the air, lost my balance, and tipped backwards, chair and all. I landed on the floor with a loud crash.

The girl coughed once more, rubbed a little cloud of ash from her face, and entirely unbothered, dusted the grey soot from her clothes. Then she bent down to me and asked with concern if everything was alright. She held out her hand to help me up. With a bright red face, I nodded hastily, stammered that everything was fine, and politely declined her help. Instead, I scrambled back to my feet by myself.

Whilst I was still dusting the soot from my skirt, she introduced herself as Leonora Fawley, known as Leo. I looked her up and down in disbelief. Behind her, the fire in the hearth had long since settled back down and was burning in its usual orange. But it almost seemed as if she had simply absorbed the actual flame from the fireplace into herself and brought it out with her. She had wild, copper-red curls that fell to her shoulders and glowed in the warm light exactly like the fire. From her face, which was covered head to toe in freckles, two enormous blue eyes beamed at me. She was wearing a plain green jumper, over which a small pendant in the shape of a shamrock dangled on a fine chain.

Emma, I can describe it no other way: she is a lot. From the very first second, she talked nineteen to the dozen. A constant, breathless stream of words that simply wouldn't stop. Normally, such a loud manner would put me to flight instantly. My brain would simply switch off to protect itself from the noise. But here, in this absurd place where everything seems strange to me anyway, something else happened. Her rapid, uninterrupted chatter simply shoved my own thoughts aside. There was quite simply no room left in my head for fear or homesickness for you, because Leo filled every spare corner with her words. I didn't have to think for myself; I could simply share in her thoughts and finally take a break from my own. As I very quickly learned, she comes from a wizarding family. So for her, this whole spooky business here is the normal state of affairs. And despite that, she was genuinely nice to me.

Yet no sooner was I standing safely on my own two feet again than something dreadful happened. She simply grabbed my hand. Without asking. My breath caught, and every muscle in my body tensed. You know how intolerable unexpected touches are to me. You were the only one allowed to do that, and even then, only if you asked beforehand. My pulse shot up. I tried reflexively to pull my arm back to free myself from her grip. But Leo was so boisterous and full of energy that my attempt to escape simply fizzled out. She held on to me undeterred and cheerfully pulled me along with her. I instantly grew so dreadfully hot that my wet clothes ought really to have dried on the spot. Had this been a cartoon, white steam would most certainly have risen from my ears in that moment. But I clamped my teeth firmly together. I forced myself to stare stubbornly at my shoes, counting my breaths in and out, so as to somehow endure her grip out of politeness.

I earnestly hope she didn't notice in that moment how cold and damp my hand was from sheer fright. Her hand was so warm and soft. When the initial shock subsided a little, for that tiny fraction of a second, she reminded me a little bit of you. Of how you always bravely protected me from Cynthia and her awful powder-puff patrol, and pulled me away from them. And suddenly, a thought occurred to me. Perhaps she could be a friend.

I suddenly heard Thomas's words from his card in my head: „Let them see what I see“. But Thomas is my brother. He has known me for eleven years and has long since grown used to me. If I truly show a stranger like Leo who I am – with all my strict rules and my fears – she will most certainly find me just as exhausting as everyone else does, and will doubtless run screaming in the opposite direction. I am dreadfully afraid of that. That is why I smothered that tiny, hopeful thought again immediately. I simply mustn't get my hopes up. You know best of all how that sort of thing always ends for me.

Be that as it may, Leo pulled me by the hand right across the room to a battered wooden table, chattering away incessantly all the while. I could roughly follow her rapid explanations, but it was an overwhelming flood of information that rained down on me in hasty sentences. Whenever I opened my mouth to reply to one of her remarks, she was already talking again. She asked countless questions – whether I was Muggle-born, how my letter had arrived, whether I had a pet, and what wood my wand would be made of – but in her excitement, she didn't even wait for my answers.

Instead, she showered me at breakneck speed with details of her world. She told me of a board game named „Wizard's Chess“, in which the tiny figures are alive and smash each other to pieces on the board. Since her parents are evidently the proprietors of a magical apothecary, she also casually mentioned ingredients that would likely cause any health inspector to turn green and back straight out the door – such as the fact that they sell thick worm mucus by the bucketful to thicken potions, or quite unabashedly weigh out dried beetle eyes with a ladle. And of a game called „Gobstones“ – which are apparently marbles that spit a foul-smelling liquid at the loser as a punishment. Then she prattled on about the outrageously high prices for pewter cauldrons and a shop named „Ollivander“, where the wands supposedly choose their owners themselves. In between, she incoherently wove in the latest political scandals from a newspaper called the „Daily Prophet“, explaining to me with wildly flailing hands why her father considers the Ministry of Magic incompetent.

Smack in the middle of this torrent of words, the landlady shuffled over to our table and brought the promised tea. I needed that peppermint so desperately right now. Yet before I could even reach for the cup, Leo leaned forward, gave it a quick sniff, and promptly hissed at me that it was knotgrass. And that knotgrass is poisonous.

My blood instantly ran cold in my veins. Was this woman trying to poison me? The landlady did grumble in response to my suspicious glare that this particular infusion was perfectly safe, but I didn't move the cup a fraction of an inch. I have made a firm resolution, Emma. I shall not consume anything else in this world that I have not prepared myself.

Whilst Leo simply dismissed the incident with the potentially poisonous tea with a shrug of her shoulders, my gaze wandered over to the cosy sitting area by the entrance. The boy I had noticed upon my arrival was still lying there. Leo followed my gaze, and before I could even blink, she had grabbed my hand yet again without warning.

It was as if a bolt of lightning had struck me right in the chest, making my knees go completely weak and wobbly for a second. Do you know that feeling when you get an awful fright and your heart drops right into your boots? Exactly that sort of cold shock shot through my entire body all at once. My heart had only just stopped pounding like mad, and now, at this sudden touch, everything inside me clenched tight all over again. I wanted to snatch my hand back instantly, but before I could even properly react, Leo had cheerfully yanked me off my chair. She didn't notice at all how rigid with fright I was, and simply pulled me merrily towards the sofa.

Leo planted herself in front of it and cleared her throat loudly twice. When that had no effect, she unceremoniously nudged the boy's foot with the toe of her shoe to wake him up. I simply stared at her with wide eyes. It was almost fascinating to watch how fearlessly and marvellously uncomplicated she went about it. She approached strangers without fretting for even a second over what was permitted and what was not, as if it were quite simply the most normal and easiest thing in the world.

The boy started awake, blinking, and looked around in confusion. Leo beamed a radiant smile at him, tossed a fleeting apology into the room, and asked in the very same breath whether we might sit down. Before he had even properly grasped the situation, he had sleepily budged up a bit. No sooner were we seated than Leo bombarded him, too, with a hailstorm of questions.

Thus I learned that his name is Colin Wheeler. He seemed very harmless at first glance. To my great horror, however, he quite unabashedly admitted amidst Leo's torrent of words that he does not like to read – which is absolutely incomprehensible to me. How can anyone not like reading? But he was decidedly friendly and proudly showed me a picture of Hogwarts he had drawn himself. In it, the school looked like an enormous, ancient castle.

He then told us, with shining eyes, that he wanted to be a „Gryffindor“. Since I had not the foggiest idea what he was talking about, I enquired. That was the perfect cue for Leo. She took a deep breath and explained the entire system of these four houses to me.

For the next forty-five minutes or so, I simply sat on that battered sofa and drank in every single word from Leo's lips. It was the very first time anyone had explained this strange world to me at all. Hitherto, it had consisted merely of shopping lists and freely dancing brooms for me. But Leo suddenly gave the whole thing a historical foundation and firm, tangible rules. I needed these explanations in that moment as desperately as oxygen, whilst Colin sank deeper and deeper into the cushions beside me and eventually simply fell back asleep.

Leo explained to me, at her previously established pace, that the school had been founded over a thousand years ago by the four most powerful witches and wizards of the age. They had built the school far away from normal folk, in order to teach magical children in safety. To organise the student body, each of the founders had established their own house – named after themselves – into which only those who shared their very own personal values and virtues would be admitted.

First came the aforementioned Gryffindor, which had been founded by Godric Gryffindor. His house stood for courage, bravery, nerve, and chivalry. It is represented by a lion. For me, that at least explained why Colin preferred to dream of chivalrous adventures rather than pick up a sensible book.

After that, she came to speak of Hufflepuff, which went back to Helga Hufflepuff and featured a badger on its crest. She had favoured loyalty, tireless diligence, tolerance, and a strong sense of fair play in her pupils. Leo emphasised that Hufflepuffs were the true backbone of the school, even if they were often quite unjustly underestimated by others. As she said this, she excitedly crossed her fingers, beamed all over her face, and told me with the broadest smile that she earnestly hoped to end up exactly there.

Seamlessly, she went on to introduce Ravenclaw, which had been created by Rowena Ravenclaw and displayed an eagle. Her house embodied learning, wit, wisdom, and pure intellect. Emma, when Leo explained this part, I could physically feel my heart beating a tiny, telltale fraction faster. An entire house, a community of its own, solely for people who prize knowledge, logic, and learning? It sounded like a utopian version of the London library.

The conclusion to her explanations was finally Slytherin, the house of Salazar Slytherin. He had valued qualities such as ambition, cunning, leadership, and a strong will to survive. Represented by a serpent. At this point, Leo dropped her voice to a conspiratorial whisper and added that Slytherin had, however, also harboured a concerning preference for pure-blood wizarding families, and that historically, most dark wizards had emerged from this house.

Afterwards, she lectured further on the school's Latin motto, which is written on the banner beneath the official Hogwarts crest: „Draco dormiens nunquam titillandus“. Leo laughed brightly as she explained to me that this means one should never tickle a sleeping dragon.

I stared at her in brief incomprehension. What on earth sort of peculiar motto is that for a school? At other schools, banners feature things like diligence or unity. And here, one is directly warned against annoying gigantic monsters.

Leo then explained to me, however, that the motto is actually merely intended to caution pupils against putting themselves in unnecessary danger or provoking slumbering, perilous forces. She said some even believe the „sleeping dragon“ might stand for the school itself. Hogwarts might appear peaceful and mysterious, but it is brimming with immense magical power and would defend itself frightfully fiercely if threatened. I thought about that for a moment. Basically, for Muggles it simply means: Let sleeping dogs lie. Only the magical version – and presumably a good deal more dangerous.

But at any rate, that solved one mystery for me. Because it finally explained the crest on the cover of this beautiful, thick leather book in which I am currently writing down my thoughts for you. When Thomas's parcel arrived this morning, I had already admired the deep embossing with the crest at length, but didn't yet know what to make of it. I had thought I should have to painstakingly look the phrase up in a dictionary. Leo, however, seemed to be excellently acquainted with every historical detail, no matter how minute, as if she had already memorised the entire school chronicle.

Just as I was about to ask who actually decided which house one fell into, and exactly by what criteria, Leo plucked the question straight from my questioning face as a matter of course. She wriggled about excitedly on the cushions and immediately revealed to me that there was an ancient magical artefact – a centuries-old pointed hat that could speak. This hat, she said, was placed upon one's head on the first evening in front of the entire school. It delved straight into one's thoughts, analysed one's character, potential, as well as one's most hidden traits, and then shouted the appropriate house aloud into the hall.

I finally gave up on trying to interject into this torrent of words; I simply leaned back and listened to her attentively. This girl truly talked without pausing for breath. Sometimes she even seemed to forget to breathe, so that it almost appeared as if she only paused her speaking to take one deep, audible gasp of air, merely to carry straight on at the very same breakneck speed immediately afterwards.

Whilst Leo, after her next deep breath, tirelessly bubbled forth further details about house ghosts and house-elves, I simply listened to her words from the cushions. The scratchy, wet clothes were forgotten for a moment. If this hat functioned even remotely as flawlessly in its analysis as Leo claimed, then there was a place for everyone at this school. And that meant, I concluded, that perhaps, just perhaps, there might be a proper place for me there too.

Whilst Leo tirelessly told me more things about Hogwarts and the wizarding world, gesturing wildly with her hands – which was a new development – my attention was drawn outside for a moment. Through the slightly ajar window next to our seating area, I heard voices. Two girls had evidently collided with each other out in the pouring rain, and one had fallen her full length. I watched as they helped one another up, hastily made certain that the other was alright, then the front door swung open and they stepped through the wooden door together.

One girl wore a plain, auburn dress. She had plaited her brown hair into a neat braid that fell over her shoulder. Her face was speckled with freckles, and she looked about her looking at least as startled and overwhelmed as I had when I arrived.

And the other girl looked as though she had strolled straight out of a picture book – at least, before she had landed out in the rain. She wore a pale, short-sleeved dress with a delicate floral pattern, which was now clinging to her soaking wet, and bore a distinct stain on the side from her fall. With it, she wore a rather conspicuous necklace with a green pendant. Her auburn hair fell over her shoulders in large, perfect waves, as if the frightful weather had not been able to touch them at all, quite unlike her dress. In contrast to the other girl, the wandering brooms, which were sweeping up tufts of cat hair in the corners entirely of their own accord, did not seem to puzzle her in the slightest.

Scarcely had the two girls entered the room when the door swung shut behind them as if by magic, locking with a loud bang. Directly afterwards, there was a rattling and clanking, as if invisible iron bolts were sliding forward entirely of their own accord inside the wood to bolt the door firmly.

The landlady behind the bar apologised for the racket and interjected casually that this mechanism was unfortunately necessary so that the „Muggles“ wouldn't disturb them. I imperceptibly knitted my brow and wondered whether these so-called „Muggles“ might be something akin to magical rats or a particular breed of vermin.

Since Leo had long since noticed from the one or two questions I had managed to interject into her bubbling torrent of words earlier that I hadn't the faintest idea about this world, she quickly leaned over to me. She whispered to me, short and sweet, that this was the term for perfectly ordinary people without magical powers, and gave me an encouraging wink. I felt a part of my inner tension release, and accepted this saving piece of information with a genuine, grateful smile.

Afterwards, the landlady called us all to the bar to complete the formal registration. Leo was already bouncing excitedly on her tiptoes again, and Colin was still battling bravely against a yawn. The two new girls stood directly beside us. The landlady introduced herself to us as Mrs Blackfield. Once again, she pulled her thick, leather-bound ledger towards her, opened it, and began to call out our names in turn.

First, she called out Colin Wheeler, whereupon the sleepy boy from the sofa gave a dutiful nod. Then it was Leonora Fawley's turn. Next, Mrs Blackfield called the name Genevieve Boot. That was the girl who looked as if she had stepped out of a picture book. She was just smoothing down her soaking wet, dirty floral dress with a perfectly elegant motion, whilst the conspicuous green pendant on her necklace sparkled in the candlelight.

After that, Klara Frieda Hoffmann was called. Now the shy girl had a name, too. She kept her hands clasped tightly together in front of her tummy. She looked somewhat uncertain and kept stealing secret glances at the others to see what they were doing. Essentially, the two of us stood rather awkwardly amongst the other three. One could probably tell just by looking at us that we hadn't a clue about this whole world. Then the woman behind the bar raised her eyes, looked directly over at me once more, gave me a smile, and finally added Eleonor Lockwood to the list.

Once this paperwork was dealt with, Mrs Blackfield clapped her hands energetically. At last, she explained to us exactly how everything would proceed: we were to stay here for two nights. The girls would sleep in the girls' dormitory, the boy in the boys' dormitory. Someone named Humfrey would collect us the following morning and escort us safely to Diagon Alley so that we could purchase our school supplies. And the day after that, he would take us to the train, the Hogwarts Express. That would be departing from Platform 9¾.

I instantly went through the layout of London's King's Cross station in my head. I know the layout precisely, rail for rail, number for number. And I came to the conclusion: there is no Platform 9¾ there. There is Platform 9 and Platform 10. Mathematically speaking, a fraction as a platform designation is an impossibility! I was just about to open my mouth to point this out to her, but Mrs Blackfield was already shooing us along. It was late and we had to be up early in the morning.

As we turned around and walked together towards the lifts, something peculiar happened. Genevieve turned and introduced herself once again most politely. Klara also shyly whispered her name, and Leo's introduction bubbled straight out again like a warm fountain of words anyway. I had to make a real effort to find my voice so that my name could be heard at all and didn't get stuck in my throat.

And then I suddenly recalled the words Thomas had written on my card: „Just do not allow them to make you invisible and rob you of this magnificent chance.“ I did not want to be invisible, Emma. I wanted to be brave and actually say something for once, just like Leo. With her, it looked so marvellously easy. So I decided to step out of my passive role as an observer and address Genevieve directly. One does need a clear beginning for a conversation, after all, a so-called icebreaker. I looked at her and said loudly and clearly: „Your dress is dirty.“

Instantly, it went completely quiet. Eight eyes stared at me all at once. I didn't understand why in the slightest. It was simply a perfectly accurate reflection of reality! Her dress was pale, and on the side clung a great, dark stain from her fall in the rain. It was a pure statement of facts, entirely without malice or judgement. I was merely pointing out the physical realities to her.

But Genevieve turned utterly venomous straight away. She looked at me as though I had hurled a dreadful swear word at her head. With an offended snort, she grabbed Klara's hand, pulled her along, and strutted off like a wounded queen. As she walked, she hastily pulled Klara towards the first lift and hissed over her shoulder that I could jolly well clean it for her if it bothered me so much.

I simply stood rooted to the spot and stared after her. I just do not understand these social things. Why is the objective truth an insult to so many people? Why do they get angry when one simply presents them with facts? After this first attempt at making contact had failed so appallingly, I sadly resolved that from now on I had better go back to doing what I do best: keeping my mouth shut. In retrospect, it was presumably not the ideal icebreaker. Why do I always say things wrong? Why do things coming out of my mouth always sound so very different from how I actually mean them?

At that exact moment, the lift door in front of Genevieve and Klara opened. But it was no ordinary lift. A sudden, violent gust of air shot out of the cabin and blew the two girls backwards so forcefully that they landed rather unceremoniously on their bottoms. Immediately, a shrivelled little head dangling from the ceiling inside began to scold them loudly, telling them to get in at once and not stand about in the way. The two of them hesitated in fright, but no sooner had they stumbled inside, swallowing hard, than the wooden box hurtled upwards at a breakneck speed. One could only hear their screams, which very quickly grew fainter and fainter the higher the lift shot.

Meanwhile, our lift door opened too, and Leo and I were practically sucked into the cabin. Emma, I assure you, what was in there had absolutely nothing to do with proper engineering. They were precarious wooden boxes hanging from coarse ropes. And the worst part: there were no buttons, nor levers, nor switches, only a musty smell of mouldy, damp wood.

They were operated by real, small, shrunken heads swaying from the ceiling. Our head suddenly spoke to us in a shrill, far too high-pitched voice. I flinched in fright, whilst Leo jerked her head upwards. The thing looked like an enormous, ancient, shrivelled grape, with deeply sunken cheeks and trembling lips. The skin stretched like leather over the tiny skull. But its eyes were alert and far too alive – they darted back and forth quite frantically and uncertainly. The string by which it was tied trembled with every word.

He asked us in his squeaky voice where we were heading. I was rigid with fright, but Leo collected herself decidedly faster than I did. Completely undaunted, she asked to be taken to the top floor. The shrunken head finally moved the wooden box – or rather: he barely moved it at all. It crept upwards so dreadfully slowly that I felt no movement whatsoever. After what felt like an eternity, Leo grew impatient and asked loudly how much longer this would take. The head merely wobbled sluggishly on its string and remarked in its squeaky voice that he just had to be careful. It was a very long way up, after all, and he didn't want anyone to get hurt. Safety first. When we asked again whether we had even moved from the spot at all, the thing said in all seriousness that he reckoned on an arrival time of 43 hours.

43 hours! Emma, that is almost two whole days! Leo looked at him in disbelief and tried to somehow hurry him along, scolding that we should have long since starved to death by then. But the shrunken head was completely unbothered. He merely looked towards the corner, where a half-unwrapped, utterly squashed, and already half-eaten chocolate bar lay on the dusty wood. We both stared in disgust at the revolting thing, but the head merely remarked drily that on such a long journey, one simply had to take what one could get.

Leo planted her hands indignantly on her hips and tried again. She explained to him that we urgently needed to sleep in order to be fit in the morning. At that, the head suddenly began to wax philosophical, saying that time is relative. Leo now became properly forceful, demanding that he kindly make time a little less relative so we could get to the top quicker. The head merely twitched its lips and said once more that he was already doing his best, but safety comes first.

Yet then Leo said something that only made matters worse. She explained to him that we urgently needed to get to Diagon Alley and then to Hogwarts. In the exact second the word Hogwarts was spoken, the lift braked with a slight jolt and came to a complete standstill. The shrunken head suddenly looked at us quite darkly, strictly refused to travel even a single inch further, and murmured eerily that Hogwarts was dangerous.

We both talked at him wildly. We tried explanations, told him of our time constraints and that our train would be leaving soon – but nothing helped. Inside that cramped box, I grew more and more anxious. Sweat formed on my forehead. I didn't want to just watch idly anymore; I wanted to be brave for once and assert myself! Surely I couldn't lose against a shrunken head on a string! So I gathered all my courage, stood up bolt upright, and tried to sound as dangerous as possible. I threatened him that I would shove that disgusting chocolate bar where the sun doesn't shine if he didn't start the lift again immediately. But the head merely gave me a cheeky wink, and pop – the bar had simply vanished. And then he even laughed at us uproariously. It was dreadful. Nothing was working, and we were well and truly stuck in this tiny wooden box.

And then I grew hot. So dreadfully hot. It felt as though the room around us was shrinking by the second, as if the wooden walls were very slowly closing in to crush me. The air grew so thin that I could barely get any into my lungs. Emma, do you remember that awful day in the school cellar, when Cynthia locked us in on purpose and turned off the light? Instantly, that horrific feeling was back again. As if an iron ring were clasping itself tightly around my chest and drawing tighter and tighter, until there was absolutely no room left for air. That was exactly how it was now in this lift.

My entire body began to tremble. I couldn't breathe. Whilst Leo was still engaging in a loud shouting match with the shrunken head and feverishly searching the walls for secret buttons, I pressed myself deep into the darkest corner. I thrust my hand into my pocket and pressed the little pebble against my thumb so hard that it truly hurt. I had to hold on to something.

Thankfully, I had my inhaler in my coat. With trembling fingers, I pulled it out and breathed in the bitter powder – as quietly as I possibly could. I earnestly hope that Leo was distracted by her row with the head and didn't notice it. That would be dreadful. No one here must know that I need this contraption. Otherwise, it is just another weakness they can use against me. Exactly as Cynthia did back then in the cellar with my crippling fear of confined spaces – when she locked us in, knowing perfectly well that I should inevitably feel quite ill down there in that tiny space.

My vision was already blurring at the edges, and I squeezed my eyelids tightly shut again and again because the walls seemed to be inexorably closing in. I had my hand buried deep in my coat pocket, clutching my smooth comfort-pebble so tightly that my knuckles ached. I rubbed my thumb incessantly over its familiar surface to somehow anchor myself to reality instead of suffocating. Therefore, I only belatedly realised that suddenly Humfrey – the name the landlady downstairs had mentioned – was standing right in our cramped lift, as if out of nowhere. He didn't hesitate for a second and put the grumbling shrunken head in its place with such a stern reprimand that it fell silent instantly. And indeed – the very next moment, the lift shot upwards with such a rapid jolt that it instantly forced Leo to her knees. Since I was already crouching on the floor anyway, I simply clawed at the wooden floorboards as the storeys rushed past us at breakneck speed, until we finally braked gently and came to a perfectly calm halt.

When the clattering grated doors finally opened, we both literally crawled out of the lift on all fours, completely dizzy from the breakneck journey. Beneath my trembling hands, I felt a thick, soft, red carpet, and the oppressive feeling of confinement very slowly began to fade.

Leo was breathing heavily, but then immediately turned to me with concern. She studied me attentively and enquired if I was quite alright. I forced myself to stand up, smoothed down my skirt as dignifiedly as possible, and assured her in an effortfully composed tone that everything was perfectly fine, of course, even if I presumably couldn't entirely conceal the slight tremble in my voice.

Leo nodded understandingly, allowed me my pride, and turned to Humfrey instead to converse animatedly with him. Thus, I at least had a brief moment to take a closer look at our saviour for the first time.

And then came the very next shock. Humfrey is not human. He is small, has shrivelled and bald skin, and possesses enormous, protruding ears that reminded me of a bat. Leo told me that he was a „house-elf“ and that her family had one too. She said that these creatures belong to a wizarding family and work there. But, thinking about it more closely, that sounded rather like slavery. It was truly disconcerting how matter-of-factly Leo spoke of it, as if it were perfectly normal for a family to simply own another intelligent living being.

Humfrey claimed to us, at any rate, that he had had to run down approximately 342,000 storeys to rescue us. Architecturally speaking, that seemed a bit much for a building of this size, but at that point, I had no desire left to question anything further.

As I slowly stood up again, I looked around the corridor. In contrast to the room downstairs, everything up here was immaculately clean, even if the light was quite dim. There were two doors in this corridor, one on the left and one on the right – those were the girls' and boys' dormitories. Right at the end of the corridor, a narrow staircase led downwards, and directly beside it was a small window. A window in front of which one saw nothing but thick, grey clouds, through which glaring flashes of lightning darted incessantly. We truly must have travelled a very long way up!

We then walked through the left door into our girls' dormitory. This room was kept plain and tidy. Four iron bedsteads stood in a row next to one another, upon each lay an accurately folded bedspread. At the edge of the room was a small sitting area with a table, a sofa, and an armchair. Even the newspapers on the table lay in a straight pile.

And Voodoo was already there! My little black tomcat was already lying on one of the beds, looking as confident as if he owned the entire building. After all the confinement and this exceedingly exhausting day, it felt so incredibly good to see something familiar. I do not know how he does it, but it is always the same: whenever everything around me sinks into chaos and I lose myself inwardly, he simply appears and finds me again.

Nox was there, too. Whilst Leo had been incessantly chewing my ear off about the wizarding world, Hogwarts, and her parents' apothecary over the past hour and a half, she had also mentioned her „Kneazle“ in a subordinate clause. Now I saw him in the flesh for the first time. According to her explanation, it is supposedly a sort of magical cat, and that describes it rather well, actually. Like Voodoo, he has an entirely black coat, but that is where the similarities end. Nox is a good deal larger than an ordinary tomcat, has peculiar, pointed tufts of hair on his ears like a lynx, and a bushy tufted tail like a lion. Fortunately, after a brief, sceptical sizing-up of one another, the two of them seemed to leave each other in peace.

Leo ran to the window to look out, and her face suddenly turned chalk-white. She spun around immediately, flashed a peculiar-looking smile in a flash, and said hastily that it was truly very cosy in here. I assume she had been frightened by something out there and was now trying to cover it up. I know that expression in one's eyes only too well from myself. But I resolved to simply accept it in silence. I absolutely didn't want to expose her in that moment, or immediately offend the next girl, when things had been going so astonishingly uncomplicatedly and well with Leo so far. So I left it at that, merely gave her a gentle nod, and did not press the matter further.

Voodoo had in the meantime sensed at once how poorly I was feeling, and jumped down from the bed to me. As he did so, however, he also immediately rubbed around Leo's legs, purring loudly. I truly believe he likes her. Voodoo simply always notices exactly who is alright and who is not. And somehow, I have the feeling that I can rely on his instincts far better than on my own.

Whilst Voodoo greeted me with a purr and rubbed soothingly around my legs, Genevieve turned to us and enquired curiously where we had been hiding for so long. Leo instantly started prattling away, recounting our dreadfully cramped and slow journey. Afterwards, Genevieve and Klara also began to relate their own experience in the other lift. Klara mostly only added something with a quiet mumble.

Their shrunken head had apparently been much more adventurous than ours. As it turned out, those two chatty heads were actually brothers. Genevieve and Klara's driver found the entire journey terribly exciting, as it happens. He giggled maniacally to himself the whole time, merely pointed out laconically to the two girls that there were paper bags on the wall in case they were sick, and then declared quite officially that he accepted no liability for concussions or bruises.

Then their lift had set itself in motion. There must have been a tremendous jolt, as if a giant, invisible arm were violently catapulting them upwards. Genevieve recounted that the wooden floor pressed firmly against her feet, whilst her stomach felt for a moment as if it were being pushed downwards. She clung to the wall as the old wood creaked ominously and the wind whistled quite shrilly through the cracks. And then they shot upwards at a breakneck speed. Klara explained that she had felt quite distinctly how illogical it was. For her too, this extreme acceleration and the travel time didn't match the architecture. Their heads grew heavy, their knees weak, and the crazy head laughed incessantly because he found this infernal pace fantastic. To the girls, it felt as though they were shooting straight through the sky.

Then came the sudden standstill. The lift braked with such a violent jolt that their bodies simply wanted to continue following the movement, and the two of them were inevitably flung upwards. Do you remember how Ms Spencer explained the principle of the „inertia of mass“ to us in physics class? She said that things once moving in a direction wish to continue doing so, until another force stops them. The lift had stopped abruptly, it's true, but the mass of their bodies simply wanted to fly inexorably onwards and upwards.

Through this force, Klara bumped her head terribly painfully against the wooden ceiling of the cabin. And since gravity famously still exists as well, she naturally had to fall back down again afterwards. Genevieve had only just managed to cling to the wall with the very last of her strength, and probably thought for a fraction of a second that she was through the worst of it. But Klara simply tumbled backwards uncontrollably like a wet sack of flour, and landed squarely on top of her. She simply dragged Genevieve down with her, and the two of them crashed onto the wooden floor as a wild, cursing tangle of arms, legs, and snagged dresses.

When the doors finally slid open, Genevieve was deeply affronted. She recounted with a snort that she had immediately given the head a piece of her mind, because one ought to be warned of such a hazard. But the shrivelled thing merely giggled cheekily and, with feigned innocence, pointed once more to the sick bags provided. As she told her tale, Klara was still rubbing her head dazedly and likewise complained about the ludicrous speed. Finally, the shrunken head had apparently just bid them a merry farewell and shoved them both quite roughly out of the cabin into the corridor with an extra powerful gust of air.

There, Humfrey was already approaching them from the narrow staircase. The house-elf politely hoped that the journey had been pleasant, to which Genevieve retorted snippily that this depended very heavily indeed on how one defined the word „pleasant“. He handed them fresh towels, which Klara accepted gratefully, and then wished to know where Leo, Colin, and I had got to. Genevieve merely frowned and replied in incomprehension that we had, after all, got into the lift right next to theirs. At that moment, Humfrey's large eyes widened in fright, and he muttered in despair that he should now presumably have to run all those many thousands of storeys back down again. As they mentioned in passing, Colin had meanwhile remained downstairs and was peacefully devoting himself to his drawings in the seating area.

Whilst the girls were still talking, the full, oppressive gravity of our situation finally dawned on me: we are sharing this room. All four of us. Plus two cats. Emma, it is my absolute worst nightmare. At home, my room was always my sanctuary. Mother and Father truly did not respect much when it came to my needs, but that one door I could close behind me, they did. And here?

There are simply far too many smells all at once, washing over me completely unfiltered. It smells of old wood and dusty wardrobes, of Genevieve's overpowering lavender water, of the damp wool of Klara's wet clothes, and of a peculiar mixture of dried herbs and warm honey that constantly surrounds Leo. Add to that the constant, unavoidable sound of three other breathing humans in the room. For heaven's sake, I earnestly hope they don't start snoring tonight as well. That would be a catastrophe. I have absolutely no privacy here. There is nowhere I can simply be, without someone looking at me or judging me. I should have liked nothing better in that moment than to crawl straight under the bed and simply be invisible. But I forced myself with great difficulty to remain sitting perfectly still on my mattress instead, whilst the girls conversed. It was surprisingly soft, almost like a thick cloud. At least that was something in this room that felt nice and didn't hurt me.

As the girls continued to chatter merrily about their lift experience, a striking detail suddenly caught my eye. Leo, Genevieve, and I – all three of us have red hair. We almost look as though we could be sisters. Only Klara, with her brown hair, breaks the pattern. For the fraction of a second, I thought this might perhaps be a genuine commonality. A first foundation upon which one might build. But that was a fallacy, of course. Hair colour is the only thing we share here. Well, and the fact that we all have Hogwarts as a common destination.

I have noticed that Klara is very quiet. She speaks very few words, and when she does, it is with a strange, harsh accent. Genevieve, on the other hand, quite obviously places an immense amount of value on her outward appearance and already seems unable to stand me on account of my honest, factual remark about her dress. And Leo, in turn, simply knows everything about Hogwarts and an unbelievable amount about magic in general. I am just sitting here, seriously asking myself: am I truly the only one here who has absolutely no idea about anything? The only one who doesn't actually belong here? The only „Muggle“ in this entire house?

Although... thinking about it more closely, I am no longer quite so certain about Klara. Downstairs in the parlour, I noticed that she was standing about just as quietly as I was. And when those bewitched brooms were sweeping across the floor by themselves, she eyed them with utmost suspicion. Someone for whom magic is a perfectly everyday occurrence would presumably have paid no mind to such a thing. My deduction, therefore, would be that perhaps she too has no idea about any of this and comes from the perfectly normal world, just as I do. The thought that someone else here might be a „Muggle“ does comfort me a little bit, at any rate.

Well, I shall try to sleep now. I wish so desperately that you were here with me now. In your loud, adventurous way, you would certainly tell me straight away that all of this is going to be absolutely topping, and that I needn't be afraid at all. And you will presumably be right.

Yours, Ella

**Addendum: 12:12 a.m.**

Me again.

I truly tried desperately to sleep, but it was quite simply impossible. It wasn't just the frightful thunderstorm raging outside, or my incessantly racing thoughts. Above all, it was these unfamiliar surroundings keeping me awake. It was simply all far too much. That heavy, strange mixture of different smells in the room hung so thickly in the air that I felt I could scarcely breathe. Added to that was the constant, uneven sound of the other three girls breathing in the dark, which practically drove me mad.

And as I lay there trying to sleep, a shrill, downright desperate cry for help suddenly rang out, accompanied by a frantic, drumming knock at the window out in the corridor. Klara and Leo miraculously continued to sleep soundly – how they manage that amidst such infernal noise is a complete mystery to me. But Genevieve was wide awake and looked just as frightened as I did in the flickering light. Voodoo, who had curled up at the foot of my bed, merely looked at me expectantly and questioningly in the pale candlelight, as if he seriously expected me to go and look into the matter.

So we grabbed a lit candle and crept cautiously into the dark corridor towards the window, with Genevieve staying close behind me the whole time, nearly treading on my heels from sheer fright. Genevieve, of all people! The girl who, on account of my observation about her dress earlier, would have preferred never to look at me again. Yet peculiarly, in the face of this eerie incident, she was suddenly no longer quite so snippy with me. Somehow, I did feel a little sorry in that moment for having offended her so much earlier, even if that had naturally not been my intention. But looking at it entirely objectively, she still needn't have reacted quite so venomously to a simple fact.

Well, the pouring rain was still raging outside. And there, upon the narrow windowsill in these dizzying heights in which we seemingly found ourselves, a scene was playing out that initially made me truly doubt whether I was awake. For a fraction of a second, I seriously wondered whether I hadn't perhaps fallen asleep from sheer exhaustion after all, and that all this was merely a dream. But if I were actually sleeping and dreaming, I presumably shouldn't be actively asking myself whether I was asleep. That pointed quite clearly to the fact that I must be awake.

I hesitantly pushed back the latch on the window and opened it, whereupon the soaking wet animal climbed inside instantly. And Emma, it actually spoke! What is more, it first gave itself a thorough shake, getting us wet in the process, and immediately became unbelievably cheeky. It puffed itself up and told me off in an impossible tone of voice, demanding to know why opening the window had taken such an age.

The next thing this prickly creature then imperiously and demandingly asked was whether this rainy „hellhole“ was finally London. It has a dreadful voice, sounding exactly like an old, rusty door hinge. And I tell no lie, it swears worse than Auntie Tunie when her pipe has dropped into the wet flowerbed yet again whilst she's weeding. The animal planted itself in front of us and insisted in an impatient tone that we absolutely must address it by the silly title of „Sir Richard“. Then it claimed to have flown here on the back of a bird – which probably explained the hard impact against the windowpane – and was now urgently seeking someone by the name of Dumbledore.

I simply stood there mutely, trying desperately to use my biological knowledge to understand how a mustelid could anatomically and cognitively be capable of human speech in the first place. My brain felt like soft jelly in that moment.

Smack in the middle of this bizarre spectacle, Colin suddenly stumbled out into the corridor, bleary-eyed with sleep. When he saw the wet, scolding animal, he was wide awake in an instant and terribly excited. Beaming, he immediately identified the rude creature as a so-called „Jarvey“. The wet weasel was less than thrilled by this, however, and in return loudly and irritably identified Colin as an insufferable „snot-nosed brat“. I truly do not know what a Jarvey is, but one thing is certain: it definitely does not feature in a single edition of *Brehm's Life of Animals.*

Since Genevieve and I wanted nothing more than to go back to bed after this fright, we somehow finally managed to persuade the blustering animal to sleep in Colin's room for the rest of the night, so that we could sort everything out in peace the next day. After that, we quickly went back into our room.

But tell me, Emma, how on earth am I supposed to sleep now, knowing perfectly well that cheeky weasels wearing hats and swearing incessantly are flying about out there? I shall blow out the candle regardless now and try to close my eyes for at least a few hours. Let us hope tomorrow will be a little more plausible again.

Good night for real this time.

Ella

<script>
document.addEventListener("DOMContentLoaded", function() {

  // --- A. Lesezeichen-Logik ---
    const saveBtn = document.getElementById("save-bookmark");
    const loadBtn = document.getElementById("load-bookmark");
    const deleteBtn = document.getElementById("delete-bookmark");

    // Generiert für jedes Kapitel einen eigenen Schlüssel
    const pageKey = "bookmark-" + window.location.pathname;

    // Prüft, ob es für dieses Kapitel schon ein Lesezeichen gibt
    const savedPosition = localStorage.getItem(pageKey);
    if (savedPosition) {
      loadBtn.style.display = "inline-block";
      deleteBtn.style.display = "inline-block";
    }

    // Was passiert beim Speichern
    saveBtn.addEventListener("click", function() {
      localStorage.setItem(pageKey, window.scrollY);
      alert('„Dein Lesezeichen wurde erfolgreich gespeichert!“');
      loadBtn.style.display = "inline-block";
      deleteBtn.style.display = "inline-block";
    });

    // Was passiert beim Laden
    loadBtn.addEventListener("click", function() {
      window.scrollTo({
        top: parseInt(localStorage.getItem(pageKey), 10),
        behavior: "smooth"
      });
    });

    // Was passiert beim Löschen
    deleteBtn.addEventListener("click", function() {
      localStorage.removeItem(pageKey);
      alert('„Das Lesezeichen wurde entfernt.“');
      loadBtn.style.display = "none";
      deleteBtn.style.display = "none";
    });

  // --- B. Fortschrittsbalken-Logik ---
  window.addEventListener('scroll', function() {
    let winScroll = document.body.scrollTop || document.documentElement.scrollTop;
    let height = document.documentElement.scrollHeight - document.documentElement.clientHeight;

    // Berechnet die Prozente
    let scrolled = (winScroll / height) * 100;

    // Verhindert, dass der Balken über den Rand hinauswächst (z.B. bei Handys)
    if (scrolled > 100) scrolled = 100;
    if (scrolled < 0) scrolled = 0;

    // Zeichnet den Balken
    document.getElementById("myBar").style.width = scrolled + "%";
  });
  // --- C. Fortschrittsbalken exakt an die Textbox anpassen ---
  // HIER BITTE DEN KLASSENNAMEN DEINER TEXTBOX EINTRAGEN (z.B. .content oder .story-container)
  const textBox = document.querySelector('.markdown-section');
  const progressContainer = document.querySelector('.progress-container');

  function matchProgressToTextBox() {
    if (textBox && progressContainer) {
      const rect = textBox.getBoundingClientRect();

      // Setzt die Position und Breite des Balkens exakt auf die Werte der Textbox
      progressContainer.style.left = rect.left + 'px';
      progressContainer.style.width = rect.width + 'px';
    }
  }

  // Beim Laden, beim Scrollen und bei Größenänderung des Fensters anpassen
  matchProgressToTextBox();
  window.addEventListener('resize', matchProgressToTextBox);
  window.addEventListener('scroll', matchProgressToTextBox);
});
</script>
