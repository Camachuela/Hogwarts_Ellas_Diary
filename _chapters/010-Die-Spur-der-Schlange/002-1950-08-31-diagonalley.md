**Sunday, the 31<sup>st</sup> of August 1950, 09:30 a.m., in Diagon Alley, outside Gringotts Wizarding Bank.**

---

Dear Emma,

The night was far too short. I lay awake for most of it anyway, staring at the ceiling. At six o'clock, there was a knock at the door. It was Humfrey, the house-elf with the bat ears, who wished to call us down to breakfast. Only Leo woke up from it. Apparently, I must have fallen asleep at some point after all.

I must note down something about Leo. She is nice. In a chaotic sort of way. She came over to my bed very quietly and woke me carefully, so as not to startle me. That was considerate. Then she fed Nox, her Kneazle. And she gave something to Voodoo, too. And at that, I went hot and cold all over. I forgot Voodoo's food. I packed everything. Absolutely everything. My socks are sorted by colour. But I forgot that my tomcat cannot hunt here. How could I possibly have forgotten that? I am a dreadful owner. Fortunately, Voodoo ate Nox’s food. But I feel quite sick with guilt.

Leo tried to wake Klara and Genevieve, but they slept like logs. So the cats took over. A pounce on the tummy is highly effective. There was a great deal of shrieking, and they complained that they now had so very little time to get ready, even though Leo's polite attempts had simply been ignored. Genevieve dashed about the room like a whirlwind to get ready. She apparently requires hours for her hair and now only had five minutes. It looked terribly exhausting to be Genevieve.

Leo had asked Humfrey to pad the lift this time. His solution consisted of sacks full of dirty laundry. And the shrunken head was gagged on this occasion. The journey was smoother, but it smelled like the laundry bag at the sports club. I tried not to breathe until we were at the bottom.

As it turned out, the landlady's name is Elenora Blackfield. Breakfast was a veritable sugar shock. There was almost nothing but sweet pastries. Klara stuffed herself with filled pancakes as if she hadn't eaten for days, and visibly enjoyed it. The others thought it was splendid, too. I merely ate a little something. My stomach was still too unsettled because of the business with Voodoo's food and the impending day in Diagon Alley.

And then, just as we stood up to go out, my heart sank right into my boots. My trunk was standing over by the wall. And I swear, Emma, it moved. Only a brief judder, but I saw it distinctly. I immediately ran through the inventory list in my head that I had drawn up whilst packing: socks, undergarments, blouses, trousers, books. There is absolutely nothing in there that ought to move of its own accord. I forcefully looked away and hoped I had merely imagined it, but I know that is not true.

We went out into the courtyard through a back door. There was another house-elf there, but we were supposed to ignore him. We stopped in front of a dustbin and a brick wall. Humfrey tapped upon specific bricks. Then the wall opened. But not like a normal door. The bricks moved individually, shifted aside, and opened a hole in the wall. Beyond it lay „Diagon Alley“.

If „the Black Tomcat“ was a sensory nightmare, then Diagon Alley is hell itself. It is brightly coloured and loud. There are a great many people in robes doing things that seem impossible. Objects hover, there is hissing and popping everywhere. It smells of gunpowder, exotic spices, and things I cannot put a name to. And there are owls flying about everywhere. In broad daylight. That, too, is certainly not described in Brehm's Life of Animals.

Humfrey left us on our own immediately. He muttered something about it being too crowded and too frantic for him, and instructed us to be back at the meeting point at 4 o'clock. And then we realised that only Leo had her shopping list with her. Leo took the lead because she is familiar with this chaos, and motioned for us to simply follow her. But I couldn't. I simply stopped dead. You know how I get when there is too much going on. When everything is too loud and too colourful, just like that time at the fairground when I hid behind the carousel. My head felt as though it were far too full and about to burst. Everything was spinning.

I believe Leo noticed. She took my hand again. My hands were freezing cold once more. Her hand was very hot and dry. And she held me so firmly that I simply concentrated entirely on that solid warmth. It was the only thing in all that noise that felt quiet and real. It was like... do you remember when we used to spin round and round in circles in the garden until we fell over, dizzy? And how one then has to hold on very tightly to the grass so that the world stops tilting. It was exactly like that.

Emma, why must I be like this? Why can I not simply be normal? I looked around whilst clinging to Leo's hand. The other girls... they were not afraid. Genevieve was beaming, her eyes enormous with wonder. Klara simply looked curious; she examined everything in a very practical manner. And Leo... Leo simply belongs here. Why am I the only one who nearly falls over just because it is loud and colourful? I should so love to be someone else. I should love to be as brave as you. Or as unconstrained and breezy as Leo. Or as pretty and confident as Genevieve. Or as calm as Klara, even if I haven't interacted with her much yet. I should love to be like the brave women in the adventure stories we read. The ones who are never afraid. Instead, I stand here trembling because too many owls are flying about. It feels so wrong to be me.

And that frightens me even more: this is only Diagon Alley, after all. Nothing more than a few shops. If this already unnerves me so much that I have to hold on to someone to keep from falling over... how on earth am I to survive at Hogwarts? That is an entire school, an enormous castle full of magic, full of strangers and laws of nature that make no sense. If I cannot even manage the very first step, how am I to climb the whole blasted staircase?

But I scarcely had time to think about it any further. For just as I was thinking that I might perhaps be exaggerating, this crazy world proved to me that my fear is entirely justified. A loud bang jolted me from my thoughts. A girl – she introduced herself as Mildren Fenwick – fired fireworks at us. She found it exceptionally amusing. Then she proceeded to fire them at herself as well. The lack of impulse control and the danger to life and limb are apparently accepted as perfectly normal here. Safety standards are practically non-existent.

Afterwards, we passed a shop that sold nothing but brooms. But no other household appliances. A gaggle of youngsters were pressing their noses flat against the windowpane. They are flying machines. I tried not to think about how a bundle of twigs and a wooden handle could possibly generate aerodynamic lift without airfoils or propulsion. It is quite simply yet another impossibility. I have stopped questioning things. My brain has simply accepted that reality is on holiday here. But I noticed something about Leo. In front of that shop window, she suddenly grew perfectly quiet. All that energy she usually has – that constant bouncing and fidgeting, as if she couldn't stand still for a second – it simply stopped. It was as if someone had flicked a switch. She looked briefly at the brooms in the window with an expression I could not interpret. For a second, she did not look like the brave, loud Leo who always carries me along with her. She suddenly looked very small. It puzzled me, because it doesn't fit the picture I had formed of her over these past few hours in the slightest. It only shows that I actually know nothing at all about her yet. And then she urged us to move on.

Shortly thereafter, we passed a sweetshop that smelled of melted sugar and fruit. Klara and Genevieve were instantly glued to the windowpane again. How can they possibly think about even more sweets after that breakfast?

And then I saw a bookshop. Books, pages made of paper, printed with ink, structured knowledge, order, facts, bound between two solid covers. It was like a blinding lighthouse in the midst of a storm. It was the first familiar thing I had seen since my arrival here. And without thinking, I did something highly untypical. I let go of Leo's hand. Just like that. The panic that usually seizes me failed to appear. I practically ran towards the shop.

Outside on a table lay a book about flying carpets. Aerodynamically, completely nonsensical as well, just like the brooms, but I wanted to examine it. Another boy reached for it at the exact same moment. Our fingers touched briefly. He was older, perhaps eighteen or so. Very politely spoken, dressed very properly, but with an intensity in his eyes that made me nervous. And Emma, I know exactly the sort of face you are pulling right now. But stop it at once. This is neither *Casablanca* nor *Gone with the Wind*. There was no dramatic music when our hands touched. He is an adult. And frankly... he is simply not the sort of person I like. I do not know exactly what sort I do like, but it is definitively not this one. He introduced himself as Tom Riddle. He said he was going to be a teacher at Hogwarts and wished to get to know his future pupils already.

Leo was at my side instantly. She very subtly slid a fraction between him and me. It gave me a pang. Exactly as you always used to do whenever Cynthia and her retinue turned up in the schoolyard. It felt safe, but sad, too. He wanted to know our names. Leo did not wish to give them. I do not know why, but I was glad of it. There was something uncannily smooth about this Tom Riddle. It was like watching an actor who had memorised his lines a little too perfectly. Every gesture seemed rehearsed; none of it felt real. But perhaps I am merely projecting my fear again. You know how I get when I am overwhelmed. My brain then looks for danger everywhere, even when there is none at all.

When we had walked away from him, I wanted to thank Leo. I felt quite stiff and awkward; I never know how to properly address such a thing. When I thanked her for her support, Leo merely shrugged her shoulders. The morning sun happened to fall exactly on her head. Her red hair suddenly glowed, almost like fire. I must have stared at it for too long and was distracted, because I almost missed what she said. She said, quite casually, that friends would do that sort of thing for one another, wouldn't they? I froze dead in the middle of the pavement. *Friends*. She used the word just like that. As if it were already an established fact.

But we have only known one another for roughly twelve hours. Twelve! And we spent most of that time asleep, to boot. How can one possibly be friends already? That is far too quick; realistically speaking, it is entirely impossible. I was utterly perplexed. I didn't know what to say, so I merely nodded and agreed with her like an absolute idiot. Fortunately, Klara and Genevieve noticed none of this. For they were still pressing their noses against the windowpane of the sweetshop, debating sugar sticks as if their very lives depended on it.

It seems I have a friend now. Or rather: Leo chose me, just as I chose Voodoo. For a moment, it felt quite warm in my chest. Not that tight feeling I usually get so often. But I mustn't be careless. I remember exactly how it was at home. Girls can be dreadfully mean. They can smell it when someone is different. Cynthia was nice at first, too, before she realised that I am the way I am. I must remain vigilant. I mustn't rely on things being different here.

Then the worst happened. Leo absolutely insisted on going into an apothecary. Klara went with her. And Genevieve went into a clothes shop opposite. And suddenly I was standing alone on the street. The noises grew louder. The faces of the people walking past suddenly seemed grotesque again. I tried to focus. I looked through the apothecary window. Leo was gesturing wildly in front of a glass jar containing a horn. A unicorn horn, as I learned later. So unicorns really do exist. Of course they do. Why on earth not. I looked over at Genevieve, who was trying on robes as if she were at Harrods and not in a magical parallel world. Her ability to simulate normality is remarkable.

I stopped in front of a pathetic puppet theatre when someone tapped me on the shoulder. I nearly jumped out of my skin with fright. It was a somewhat older girl, very nice, very Scottish. She introduced herself as Minerva McGonagall. And she was completely different from that Tom Riddle earlier. Not so smooth. Not so... rehearsed. She seemed genuine. She held out her hand to me in greeting. Normally, I freeze at that sort of thing, but with her, I dared to. I shook her hand. She was very nice and pointed out that we had missed the wand shop on our stroll, and that wands were free for pupils.

Free. The word echoed in my head like a wrong note. Father always says: „If someone wants to give you something for nothing, look for the catch.“ Or: „There is nothing that is truly free.“ A wand must surely be expensive to manufacture. The materials, the labour time, that specialised knowledge... and simply giving that away? That is quite simply a ruinous business model. Who pays the craftsmen? How does this shop finance itself? It contradicts everything I have ever learned about economics at Father's dining table. This entire magical world seems to be built on principles that would drive a businessman to madness.

But I didn't say that aloud, of course. I thanked her, probably much too quietly again, and hurried back to the others.
Genevieve and I then collected the others from the apothecary. Leo was ecstatic about the horn. We decided to go to the wizarding bank, „Gringotts“, first. An enormous, white building that stands entirely crooked and has a gigantic dragon on its roof. The architecture here is a single affront to structural engineering.

Inside the bank, we encountered two boys. They looked at us the way Cynthia always used to look at us. Like dirt beneath their shoes. They said something about our smell. Klara, who had scarcely spoken a word until then, simply tripped one of them up. I was, admittedly, a little impressed. When the boys became confrontational, Genevieve stepped in front of Klara, brave as a lioness, and tried to shoo them away. Then one of the boys said a word. „Mudblood“. I do not know the word. It isn't in any of my books. But I know the tone of voice. It is the tone of voice one uses to describe vermin before crushing it. I do not know exactly what it means, but I know it applies to me. I am the one who doesn't belong here. And he is presumably right about that.

At the bank, we were served by goblins. Small, shrivelled creatures with long, thin fingers, cold, dark eyes, and very pointed teeth. I tried not to stare. They are terrifying.

A goblin at a counter explained the terms for the starting capital to us. It is 300 Galleons per person. And now brace yourself: we do not have to pay any interest. None at all. Zero percent.

Father would go purple in the face if he heard that. He would say it is „commercial suicide“. A bank lends money in order to get more money back, surely. But these people are lending us money for seven years, until we finish school, and want nothing extra in return? That is utter balderdash. How on earth do they pay their bills? Or the goblins? How can the bank even exist if it earns nothing? None of this fits with what Father is always going on about at dinner when he is ranting about „the economy“. This entire magical world seems to operate by rules that make no sense.

But once again, I asked no questions. I do not wish to stand out any more than I already do. The goblin asked for our names and pointed a long, claw-like finger at the air in front of us. Suddenly, a contract written in glowing ink appeared there. We simply had to sign in the air using a – wand, I presume. It felt much more binding than ink on paper.

Meanwhile, the goblin serving us was staring at Genevieve's emerald necklace, with a hunger in his eyes that gave me goosepimples. But it didn't stop at staring. He wanted to touch it with his long, creepy fingers and started asking questions. Peculiar, far too specific questions in his scratchy voice. I mean, he knows the necklace. It was as if he were seeing an old, unpaid bill. Genevieve, who usually acts as though she owns the world and whom nothing fazes, took a step back. Her hand flew to her neck very quickly, as if she wanted to protect the necklace she had received from her grandmother for starting school from his gaze. She looked exactly as creeped out as I felt.

This is no ordinary bank. It doesn't just manage numbers; it manages old histories, too. And the goblins seem to have a very long, very unforgiving memory. But do you know what the eeriest part was? It reminded me of home. The goblins have exactly that same cold, empty look I know from my father and his business partners. When they look at you, you don't feel like a person, but like an object whose value is currently being appraised, completely soullessly. In the City, those people wear suits to hide it. Here, they have pointed teeth and claws. But it is the selfsame coldness, only packaged more honestly.

Be that as it may: I now have 300 Galleons. And an enormous problem. How on earth am I supposed to earn such a sum as a schoolgirl, even without interest? I haven't the faintest idea how I am to manage that. I urgently require a repayment plan, otherwise I shan't be able to sleep peacefully. That would be the first thing Father would demand.

But before I think about repaying it, we must first spend the money. We must now work our way through Leo's shopping list. First of all, we need school clothes. I am certain Genevieve will be at my side as a stylistic advisor for this – whether I like it or not. She has that very specific, critical eye when it comes to clothes. And then... books. Emma, there are heaps of books on the list! I am looking forward to that the most. And in addition, loads of other alchemical paraphernalia that I haven't the faintest idea what it is or what it will be good for. Cauldron, scales, wand.

But it will be a challenge. I can already see that my primary task will consist of preventing Klara and Genevieve from investing their entire starting capital straight away in exploding sweets, joke articles, and new silk cloaks. I shall likely have to take on the thankless role of spoilsport, reminding them to be sensible with their money. 300 Galleons sounds like a lot, but without budgetary discipline, it will vanish quickly too.

And as I write this, Emma, my brain is already starting to calculate again. It is like an automatism. I am already pondering where we can save on equipment. Perhaps buying second-hand instead of everything new? Mother would probably box my ears for that thought. „Lockwoods do not buy second-hand, Eleonor,“ I hear her voice in my head, very pointed and indignant. „It is beneath our dignity.“ But Mother is not here. And Mother doesn't owe 300 Galleons to (vengeful?) goblins.

If we go about this cleverly, there might perhaps be a surplus. And immediately I am wondering how one might sensibly invest it here to generate a return. If Gringotts won't pay interest, there must surely be other investment opportunities. Somehow, this money must work for me so that I can repay the loan.

Oh God, Emma. I sound like Father. It is terrifying. I always thought I was different from him, but all those years of having to listen to his monologues on markets and risk management at dinner have apparently left deeper marks than I thought. His influence runs deeper than I care to admit. But perhaps... perhaps this pragmatic, cold part of him is the only thing that can help me here.
But I must get on with the shopping for now. The girls are waiting already. I shall write again this evening and tell you how the rest of the day went.

Yours, E.

**Addendum: Sunday, the 31<sup>st</sup> of August 1950, 9:38 p.m., at „The Black Tomcat“, 22 Charing Cross Road, London.**

---

Emma, silence at last.

You cannot believe how precious silence can be when one has spent the entire day in a sensory hurricane. I am sitting on the wide windowsill of our room at „The Black Tomcat“ inn. It is dark outside, and Diagon Alley is asleep – or, at least, it is pretending to be.

I have created a little island of normality for myself here on the windowsill. Your battered copy of F.H. Burnett’s A Little Princess lies open upon my lap. I have spent the past hour doing nothing but reading those familiar sentences, simply to calm myself.

It is peculiar how strongly the story of that girl resonates, today of all days. You know the parallels, after all. She in her cold garret, I in my no less cold home in Harrow. They ought to have been here today. That is what normal parents do, isn't it? They take their children to Diagon Alley. Everywhere in this hubbub today, I saw proud parents placing their hands upon the shoulders of their beaming children, supporting them. And I wondered what that must feel like. Not being the disappointment that has to be hidden away from the employer because she doesn't fit the picture. It truly hurts. More so than I actually care to admit.

That is why I need this book so desperately right now. Because of the way Sara Crewe survives in the attic. When the world treats her like dirt, she inwardly straightens her imaginary crown. She uses her imagination as a shield against the cold and the cruelty. When she imagines herself to be a princess, it is not out of vanity, but to preserve her dignity. I know that feeling. I try to do the same every day. When the world is too loud, or my parents' looks are too disappointed, I retreat into my own head. Imagination is simpler and more comforting than reality – regardless of whether that reality consists of cold London drawing rooms or chaotic, magical Diagon Alleys.

The other girls are already asleep. It is a peaceful picture that does not fit the chaos of the day at all. I can hear the deep, steady breathing of Klara and Genevieve – the latter of whom, incidentally, looks outrageously elegant even in her sleep; however does she manage it? Leo, on the other hand, is the absolute opposite: she mutters something now and then, and subsequently twitches violently with her hands and feet. I wager she is already dreaming of magical exercises and waving her new wand in her sleep. Curled up at her feet is her Kneazle. He appears as though he were sleeping, yet every now and then a yellow eye opens and fixes upon me watchfully, before dropping shut again. I should be most interested to know what this magical creature makes of us, Voodoo and me.

As I sit here, with the book upon my knees and the three strange girls in the room, the parallel to Sara Crewe feels even stronger. For even Sara was not entirely alone in her cold garret in the end. You know, of course, that she too found unexpected allies in a world that had effectively cast her out. Just remember the slow but loyal Ermengarde; the little, temperamental Lottie; and Becky, the scullery maid, who was every bit an outcast as she herself was.

When I look at my roommates - they are all so vastly different, and I am still scarcely able to gauge them - I nevertheless wonder whether this might be my version of that story. I do not yet know which of them plays which part, or whether they even wish to be part of my story at all. But today... today they simply took me by the hand when the noise became too much. They did not treat me like a problem that had to be shoved aside, but like someone who simply required assistance navigating. I have garnered a rather naïve little hope that here, in this magical garret, I too might find friends who like me, even though I am the way I am.

Be that as it may, I have a dull feeling that this was not the last evening I should have to flee to Sara's garret to escape the noise in my own head. I suspect I shall require this refuge rather frequently in the future, and I am infinitely glad to have your scribbled-upon copy with me.

Voodoo, incidentally, has decided that now is his time. He is winding himself about my legs, purring, pressing his head against my hand and demanding to be stroked. You know very well I did not choose him; he simply decided one day that I now belonged to him. And cats are never wrong about that sort of thing.

At any rate, I am enjoying this vibrating purr and the few minutes in which, for once, nobody wants anything from me. It is as if I must first take a very deep breath and wait for all the stirred-up dust of the day to settle in my head before I am ready for tomorrow.

But I did promise you in my last entry to recount the remainder of our shopping expedition before I go to sleep. And frankly: if I do not write it down now, I shan't find any peace anyway. For my head still feels exactly like a beehive that someone has been poking at with a stick.

Speaking of sticks: that brings me directly to Mr Ollivander, and the great disappointment concerning what we are actually supposed to perform magic with here. But first things first:

The walk to Mr Ollivander's wand shop was once again... well, you know the drill by now. Loud, crowded, chaotic. But instead of panic, I slowly began to feel more of an annoyance. Faced with too many stimuli, my brain simply digs its heels in eventually. So as not to lose one another in the crush, we formed a sort of crocodile. Genevieve took Klara's hand, and Leo took mine again. It was lovely to see that Genevieve took the lead for Klara. I do still scrutinise Klara from the side occasionally, because I simply cannot make head nor tail of her; she is like a closed book. But it comforts me to know that she has someone to pull her along just as I do, in case she feels just as inwardly overwhelmed as I do. Because if I am entirely honest: without Leo, I should be utterly lost here. I should probably have long since been sitting in a corner somewhere between cauldrons and owl droppings, bawling my eyes out. Instead, I can simply allow myself to be pulled along by this red ball of energy, who seems to know exactly what's what around here. It was somehow comforting to be nothing but a carriage in this peculiar little train, and not have to think for myself.

Outside the shop, we crossed paths with two children who were proudly showing off their new wands. I was... well, underwhelmed. Frankly, I had expected something else. I do not know exactly what I expected, but perhaps something a little more like the ones in the fairy-tale books. Instead, the things looked like crooked twigs one might pick up off the ground in Hyde Park.

Ah well. We trooped into the shop in single file. And Emma, this shop was a salvation. It was suddenly dead silent. My ears fairly buzzed, because the noise from outside was so abruptly gone. The shop was tiny, dusty, and looked rather like an exploded shoe shop. Everywhere, these shoebox-sized boxes were stacked right up to the ceiling; the shelves were lopsided and crooked. I do believe there is not a single thing in here that is truly straight. My inner stickler for order was screaming, but at the same time, this quiet was simply heavenly.

Mr Ollivander is... intense. He lined us up and stared at us as if he were x-raying our souls. Then he drifted through the shelves, muttering. It truly seemed as though he were talking to the wands – and as though they were answering him. I found it unbelievably creepy.

Leo received hers first. When Ollivander handed the fine piece across the counter to her, I thought immediately that it suited her perfectly in visual terms. It was unusually long, and crafted from an extremely pale, almost radiant birch wood. Despite its length, it appeared incredibly delicate and dainty. The slightly thicker handle looked as if it would nestle gently into the palm of one's hand, and was covered head to toe in gossamer-thin, artfully carved vines. It looked almost as if a wild honeysuckle had lovingly twined itself around the wood.

And that is a truly splendid fit, Emma. For I know honeysuckle rather well, because Aunt Petunia used to have it in the garden. Do you remember? The neighbours used to get frightfully upset about it on a regular basis, because the vines rampaged everywhere and, to their fussy eyes, looked simply untidy and entirely unkempt. Fortunately, my aunt did not care a jot. It fascinated me so much at the time – a plant that simply stubbornly refuses to adhere to the strict, stuffy garden norms of suburbia – that I immediately read up on everything about it in the library.

Botanically speaking, honeysuckle truly does grow entirely chaotically and irrepressibly in all directions. It clambers over every obstacle and refuses to be forced into orderly paths. Yet at the same time, it has these exquisite blossoms that smell incredibly warm and inviting.

I read in an old book on the language of flowers back then what that actually signifies. Precisely because of this climbing quality, the way honeysuckle embraces and clings to walls or trees, it is an ancient symbol of unwavering loyalty and deep emotional attachment. Because it grows so persistently, it stands for reliability, for unconditional devotion, and even for protection and security. So, in principle, wild, irrepressible chaos that nonetheless wraps itself unwaveringly and protectively around its loved ones, paired with a sunny disposition – that is basically Leo in plant form.

Mind you, she was trembling so much with excitement that she promptly dropped this delicate work of art at first, and it showered sparks loudly upon the floorboards. She was dreadfully embarrassed. But when she took it properly in her hand, something happened. Emma, you should have seen her. All her fidgeting ceased. She suddenly stood perfectly still, and it seemed as though she had... arrived. As if a missing puzzle piece had clicked into place. From this new, unfamiliar calm, she swished the wand through the air – it looked like a large „U“. And immediately, the candle on Ollivander's desk flared up brightly. Ollivander was delighted. He muttered the facts – birch wood, 14 inches, with a core of jackalope antler – and then explained with shining eyes what that signifies.

I know you are doubtless asking yourself right now why I am writing down the technical specifications of a stick for you. But you must understand, this is not like being in a department store where one simply picks out the prettiest umbrella.

The whole thing follows a complex, almost scientific system – even if the variables sound absurd. Ollivander explained that different types of wood possess their own „personalities“. (Yes, Emma, I am currently writing about the character of dead wood. I know.) The wand apparently chooses the wizard whose nature matches its own. Inside, there is a magical core; that is essentially the engine that amplifies the wood's magic or endows it with specific abilities. And even the length is decisive: longer wands tend towards people with „large personalities“ for a rather sweeping, dramatic style of magic, whilst shorter wands stand more for elegant and refined spellwork. It is, therefore, character analysis by means of matter.

And this particular wand (long, birch, jackalope antler) apparently belongs to someone who is full of the joys of spring and possesses an entirely natural instinct for favourable opportunities and coincidences. He said that luck is practically on her side; she merely has to learn to consciously use it to her advantage. When I consider her manner thus far, how she simply seems to breeze through life, it fits perfectly.

And then it was my turn. Emma. I do not know how to describe this to you. Ollivander handed me a shorter wand, about 11 inches, crafted from pale maple wood. It was not merely smooth, but beautifully wrought, with a winding, almost organic spiral shape that was reminiscent of a climbing plant and moulded perfectly to my hand. When my fingers touched the wood... it was not like touching a simple stick. It was as if a warm wave were washing up through my arm into my chest. It tingled, but in a good way. As if the wand were greeting me. It felt... right. For the first time that day, I had the feeling that perhaps I wasn't entirely in the wrong place here after all.

But then Ollivander pulled the rug out from under my feet. He muttered that the core was of Kneazle whisker, which stood for exceptionally good intuition, particularly when it came to seeing through lies or ill intentions. He said my magic reacted strongly to the emotions of others, and I ought to trust this wand, for it would show me what was right. And then he looked me straight in the eye and said he saw my „second sight“. That I could finally show my true self here. My heart nearly stopped. Why would this strange old man say such a thing? I merely stared at him with enormous eyes. When I was supposed to swish the wand, I was so flustered that I completely bungled it, and he quickly took the wand away from me again before I could break anything. But that warm, tingling sensation remained.

And then came Klara's turn. She was just as speechless as I was when Ollivander handed her her wand – a 13-inch piece of hazel wood. But where my wand looked almost elegant, hers appeared much more raw and primeval. Hesitantly, I placed my hand on her back and stroked it very carefully. I don't know if that is how one does it. But I thought to myself: if I were so distraught right now, what should I want? No clever advice, no questions. Just peace, and someone who is there and doesn't go away. So I was simply there.

When Klara and Genevieve returned to us at the step, they seemed peculiarly pleased with themselves. Genevieve because of her tough negotiating tactics, and Klara because of the encounter with the fluffy kleptomaniac. And Leo... she had composed herself again, outwardly at least. The sobbing had stopped. But Emma, looking at her sitting there, with her tear-stained eyes and slumped shoulders, I was instantly reminded of what I had written earlier.
Do you remember? That she is like a soft cushion in a room full of sharp edges, and that I was afraid someone like that would quickly be eaten alive here.

That is exactly what had just happened. I believe Leo was not only deeply disappointed by the lying girl in that moment. I believe she was also deeply disappointed by her own naivety. She looked as though someone had just forcibly ripped the rose-tinted spectacles from her nose and forced her to see the world as I often see it: as a place where kindness is exploited, and where there is often a catch behind assistance. It hurt me to see that. It was the moment the fairy-tale character realises that the real world is cruel. She no longer seemed angry, but rather... disillusioned. As though she had just learned a very hard, very expensive lesson that a good heart alone is sometimes not enough.

After the light-fingered Niffler chaos, we desperately needed a change of scenery. We moved on to the apothecary to tick the glass phials and the scales off the list.

The smell in there... it was a mixture of pungent, dried herbs and something metallic. I didn't take much of it in; my head was still too full of the theft. But I remember exactly how Leo's entire face positively collapsed further in on itself when she heard that the unicorn horn had just been sold. She had been so looking forward to seeing it once more. My heart ached for her after the fiasco with the Niffler girl.

Whilst the others paid, I crept along the shelves a little. The things they sell here... it is incredibly fascinating and thoroughly revolting at the same time. It has nothing to do with a pharmacy as we know it. There were open barrels filled with neon-green, viscous slime – „Flobberworm mucus, extra thick“, according to a handwritten sign – that bubbled quietly when one walked past. Thousands of tiny, black, glittering beetle eyes stared at me from enormous apothecary jars. Bundles of dried herbs hung from the ceiling, looking like the knotted, shrivelled hands of goblins, and in a specially secured glass case I saw shimmering skins that had probably once belonged to a Boomslang, right next to jars of pickled dragon liver. And the prices! So much money for a jar full of dead insects.

When we were outside again, a chilly wind had picked up. Without having agreed upon it, we all pulled our new black robes from the paper bags and put them on directly over our normal clothes.

This might sound silly now, but the moment that heavy, black fabric settled upon my shoulders, something happened to me. It was more than just clothing. It suddenly felt like armour. I find it hard to describe, but I suddenly felt a tiny bit less naked and exposed. It was as though the fabric itself radiated a subtle sort of magic that made me feel a little bit safer. I felt... stronger.

Perhaps it was this new armour that helped me summon the courage for the next shop: „Potage's Cauldron Shop“.
And Emma, after the screaming madness at Madam Malkin's, this place was pure peace of mind for me. It was rather dark inside, illuminated only by the glow of a large log fire in the corner. Cauldrons of every conceivable size were stacked in wobbly towers right up to the ceiling, but it seemed peculiarly tidy. It smelled of cold metal and soot. But the best part were the quiet, monotonous sounds. A rhythmic whoosh-puff came from a pair of bellows working themselves by the fire. This was joined by the slow, deep scraping of an enormous ladle in a gigantic cauldron standing next to the entrance, stirring itself.

To others this might seem gloomy or dull. For me, it was like balm for my overstimulated nerves.
Encouraged by the quiet in here and the comforting, heavy weight of my new robe-armour, I actually dared to detach myself a few yards from the group. I walked over to a heavy wooden shelf and stared at the various cauldrons – copper, pewter, brass.

Then the realisation hit me: I had no idea what size or material we actually required as first-years. Leo had the list. With a quiet sigh, I was just about to turn around to look for Leo.

And Emma, I swear to you, this girl must possess telepathic abilities. I hadn't even completed the turn before she was suddenly standing silently beside me, as if she had sensed that I needed her. She enquired in a very quiet whisper whether I had spotted anything suitable yet. The relief was immense. But the standard pewter cauldrons we evidently needed were stacked far too high anyway. We had to go to the till and ask for assistance.

Whilst we waited, I saw out of the corner of my eye how Klara leaned very far over the edge of the enormous, self-stirring cauldron out of curiosity, to look inside. I do not know what she saw – or smelled – in there. But the next moment, she jerked her head back as if she had been struck, and quickly took a few steps back. She suddenly looked a little pale around the gills. I decided it was best not to ask what was being brewed in there.

The shop assistant was an elderly witch who looked as though our mere presence were a personal insult to her. She didn't even stand up. She merely waved her wand sluggishly, and four cauldrons floated down from the ceiling stack, landing in front of us with a heavy clonk. So much magic, and yet the people here are bored by it. It is absurd.

With minimal effort, she then managed to talk us into buying a special bag with an „Extension Charm“. If I have understood this correctly, the bag is bigger on the inside than on the outside. The physics here are driving me to distraction, but the thought of always being able to carry an entire library about without giving myself a hernia was too tempting. We all took one.

As we were paying, the bored assistant made a vague gesture towards a display of blue phials on the counter. In a monotonous voice, she rattled off an obviously rehearsed spiel about this „Bluejuice Potion“ being indispensable for magical energy. Klara showed interest immediately. But Leo interceded. With the dry authority of an apothecary's daughter, she whispered to us that the stuff here was dreadfully overpriced. A good thing we have Leo with us. I should probably let them talk me into buying any old nonsense here.

Afterwards, we passed a shop named „Eeylops Owl Emporium“. The shop window was crammed with owls. Hundreds of large, unblinking, yellow eyes that seemed to follow our every move. It made me nervous. And the smell wafting from the open door... a warm, stuffy cloud of old dust, feathers, and something that smelled suspiciously of dead mice. I was very glad we didn't have to go in there.

As I eyed the animals somewhat sceptically in passing, my personal magical encyclopaedia activated instantly. Leo explained quite casually that owls are not simply pets here, but the postmen of the wizarding world. She said they are highly intelligent and actually understand spoken English! Furthermore, they supposedly possess a magical ability to find any recipient, no matter where they are hiding. Fascinating, but somehow also a little creepy that one cannot hide from the post anywhere.
But then, Emma. Then we finally stood outside „Flourish and Blotts“.

If Madam Malkin's was hell, then this was heaven. Or at least a highly overwhelming precursor to it. It is the principal bookshop for Hogwarts, and the shop is enormous. Shelves stretching right up to the ceiling, which one could scarcely see, so crammed with books that it felt as though the walls were constructed of pure knowledge. It was busy, but it was a different sort of noise to the clothes shop – a bustling, respectful rustling of paper, hushed conversations, the scent of old parchment and printer's ink. I simply love the smell of books. There is nothing finer.

I believe my brain short-circuited momentarily. I simply stopped dead right in the doorway, my mouth probably hanging wide open, blocking the path. I could not move. The sheer volume of information stored here utterly staggered me – in the best possible way.

And Emma, that wasn't all. All around me, things were happening that are actually impossible. I saw customers browsing through thick tomes and turning the pages without so much as touching them – a little flick of the wand, and the paper rustled onward all by itself. And further back, at the shelves that reached right up to the ceiling? Books were simply floating down. Quite gently and carefully, like feathers, until they landed safely in people's hands. It was like a beautiful, silent ballet of paper.

I only came to my senses when Leo gently nudged me forward from behind so that she, Klara, and Genevieve could enter too. As she squeezed past me, I caught her eye. She wasn't annoyed by the blockage at all. She looked... happy. Almost a little admiring, as if she were enjoying seeing me so utterly, speechlessly thrilled by books.

You know, Emma, that was a very peculiar moment for me. Normally, I am the one standing on the sidelines, observing the others as if under a microscope. I analyse their feelings because I often do not understand my own. But suddenly, I was the subject of observation. Leo is familiar with all of this. To her, flying books are probably as normal as bicycles are to us. But she looked at me as if she were rediscovering all this magic anew through my eyes. And she simply seemed genuinely pleased that for once today I didn't look stressed, frightened, or angry, but simply... enthralled. I felt somehow caught out experiencing a genuine emotion. I instantly felt the heat rush to my cheeks, and merely returned a sheepish smile before quickly looking down at the floor.

I allowed myself to drift towards the nearest shelf, utterly staggered by the sheer mass of history I was missing. It was overwhelming. There stood thick, leather-bound tomes with titles such as „The Founding of the Four: A Millennium of Hogwarts“ or grim treatises on „1692: The Retreat into the Shadows – The International Statute of Secrecy“.

One entire shelf in particular seemed to concern itself solely with wars. You know, Emma, a small, naïve part of me had probably hoped that a society capable of performing miracles would be wiser than us „normal“ people. That magic meant one was above such things. But then I saw a whole shelf full of books about a certain Gellert Grindelwald and a „Global Wizarding War“. The dates on the spines all ended in 1945.

A heavy, grey resignation washed over me at that. It is exactly the same as with us. Whilst we were hiding from the bombs in London, they were evidently fighting each other with wands here. It seemingly never stops, does it? Whether with tanks or magic, in the end people always end up just bashing each other's heads in. This world is more colourful and louder, but it doesn't appear to be any more peaceful.
I felt quite sick at the thought.

And then I saw it. A name on a dark red cover. It was the only name that held any recognition value in all the madness of the past 24 hours. Leo had already mentioned it a few times, and even that talking weasel Sir Richard had: Albus Dumbledore.
The book was entitled „The Twelve Uses of Dragon's Blood“, written by a certain Nicholas Flamel and that very same Albus Dumbledore.
I hadn't even read the blurb before Leo materialised beside me out of nowhere. And off she went again, full of excitement. Emma, this girl is a walking encyclopaedia of magical knowledge. She began to tell me about Flamel's alchemy and Dumbledore's discoveries without pausing for breath. And the best part? I loved it. Her eyes shine as she speaks, her hands gesture wildly, and her enthusiasm is simply infectious. It is so refreshing to meet someone my age who actually possesses a thirst for knowledge and isn't just interested in trivial nonsense.

To give my eyes something to do whilst I listened to her – eye contact is still difficult for me – I leafed absent-mindedly through the book. I learned quite by the by that dragon liver is used for various potions, and that dragon dung, when stirred into a cauldron, is a fantastic, albeit foul-smelling, fertiliser that makes plants grow unnaturally fast. Who would have thought?
Leo was just finishing her lecture on Dumbledore and that Grindelwald from the Wizarding War and turned around. And then there was a bang. It was deafening in the relative quiet of the shop. A large, heavy book had slammed flat onto the floor. For the second time today, every head turned in our direction, and it went dead silent. Leo instantly flushed that painful beetroot colour again. She truly has a talent for drawing unintentional attention to herself.

I stood a little way off and watched how she positively shrank into herself. It was peculiar to see her so agitated. Just as I was about to go over to her, a stern-looking assistant came darting over and very sharply instructed her to please treat other people's property with more care. That was really rather mean. It wasn't on purpose, after all.
When the assistant had finally gone, I went over to her. We stood there all alone between the high shelves, almost as if in a small, quiet cave made of books.

I enquired very carefully what was worrying her so. But Leo wouldn't or couldn't spit it out. She stared intently at the toes of her shoes, kneaded her hands nervously, and only managed disjointed scraps of words about brooms. Flying brooms. It was obvious that the subject caused her almost physical pain. I briefly put two and two together and asked her very gently whether it might be the height. But she didn't even nod or confirm it. She merely withdrew into herself even further, evaded my question entirely, and simply looked miserable. Her silence and her hedging were answer enough.

So I simply acted without thinking about it for long – my brain would probably have talked it to death anyway. I took her hand. I only wanted to signal to her that it was alright and she owed me no explanation whatsoever. That we would simply let the subject rest.
It was a rather peculiar contrast. Only this morning, she had stood in front of that thief like a lioness. And now, after this whole disaster, she suddenly seemed very small and crumpled. Her face was still blotchy, and I felt awfully sorry for her in that moment. She seemed incredibly relieved that I didn't probe the wound any further, but was simply there.

Our moment was interrupted by a boy coming around the corner. He asked if the book that had just fallen was the one on the theory of flight.
Leo immediately lied – and exceedingly poorly, at that – saying the book was kept somewhere else entirely. I don't know why she did that; panic, perhaps. I didn't question it. Since I had seen where the assistant had put it, I simply pointed towards the end of the shelf.
The boy fetched it and peculiarly began to prattle away immediately. He admitted that he was terribly afraid of flying on a broomstick. His plan was evidently to bypass the flying lessons through pure theoretical knowledge, in the hope that the instructors wouldn't force him to actually take off.

Leo's eyes widened. She seemed quite interested in this possibility and admitted her own fear of flying. I think she is hoping for exactly the same loophole.
He introduced himself as Benedict Moorfield, but completely ignored me as he did so and simply kept talking, even though I was standing right next to him. Leo shot me a glance – she obviously found him odd too, despite their shared phobia. We decided to make ourselves scarce and finally purchase our own schoolbooks.

I quickly asked the assistant where we might find the telescopes, the last item on the shopping list. He sighed and said they were to be had this year at „Gambol and Japes Wizarding Joke Shop“, the joke shop down the street, but that we ought to pay close attention so as not to buy trick telescopes.
I truly did not want to leave. I could have spent days in that shop. But Humfrey was waiting, and Leo was starting to look very tired; no wonder after today's emotional marathon. She saw my sad face and nudged me. She assured me that the school library at Hogwarts has dimensions that make this shop look like a tiny shoebox by comparison. There would supposedly be more books there than I could possibly read in seven years. That was actually a rather good comfort.

Only now, when I could finally tear myself away from the shelves, did we notice that Klara and Genevieve had vanished without a trace. But we didn't have to guess for long where they were hiding. Leo merely grinned tiredly and nodded towards the brightly coloured shop across the street.
I really ought to have realised that. The two of them have such a pronounced sweet tooth that they had been badgering us all day to finally be allowed in there. They had probably just been waiting for the moment we were distracted to gorge themselves.
So we moved on. And indeed, scarcely had we crossed the threshold of the bookshop when the doors of the brightly coloured shop opposite practically flew open.

Klara and Genevieve practically tumbled out into the open, and one could see the massive sugar shock from a hundred yards away. Klara wore her loot right on her face. Her entire lower half of her face shimmered in a sticky, neon pink, as if she had won a fight against candyfloss – and eaten it as a reward. It positively glittered on her skin, and she was still rapturously licking her lips, whilst individual sugar crystals sparkled in the sun. It looked marvellous. Genevieve, on the other hand, who usually appears so composed, was giggling so hysterically and at such a high pitch that she had to link arms with the sticky Klara so as not to lose her balance. An almost visible cloud billowed around the two of them, smelling so intensely of artificial strawberry, peppermint, and burnt caramel that my teeth almost ached simply from breathing it in. They seemed completely over-excited, like two clockwork toys that have been wound up far too tightly and are now whizzing wildly about.

Together, accompanied by incessant giggling, we then headed for the joke shop „Gambol & Japes“. I noticed that Leo once again gave a very wide, deliberate berth to the shop displaying racing brooms in its window. The fear must run truly deep if she cannot even look at them through a pane of glass.

Whilst Genevieve once again stopped, utterly fascinated, in front of the talking skeleton that was still cracking the same flat jokes, I spotted a wobbly revolving stand with so-called „joke books“ outside next to the door.

I opened one out of pure curiosity, but regretted it instantly. Emma, it is beyond my comprehension. That people actually waste valuable resources on such intellectual rubbish. Paper, ink, binding – all for jokes that are so flat and lacking in class that they could slip beneath a door crack. It was an absolute waste of materials. After the wonders I had just witnessed in the bookshop, this seemed like an insult to anyone capable of reading.

Inside, it was... an experience. Imagine a fireworks factory exploding in the middle of a nursery school, and you have a rough idea of the chaos that hit us.

Total anarchy reigned. The air was thick and stuffy, an almost impenetrable wall of gunpowder, burnt rubber, and artificial raspberry flavouring. I had to press my back flat against a shelf and throw my arms protectively over my head, because a wild salvo of magical fireworks suddenly went off. But not beautifully up in the sky, oh no. Hissing, sparking rockets tore horizontally through the aisles at eye level like crazed insects made of light. It was a crossfire of neon colours. A particularly aggressive, purple howler hissed mere fractions of an inch past my nose, left a trail of foul-smelling smoke, and ricocheted like a stray bullet against the opposite wall, where it exploded in a cloud of glitter.

And the most absurd thing wasn't even the projectiles. I actually saw children – real, living children – hovering just below the high ceiling. A boy was drifting up there, puffed up round as a ball like an enormous, pink balloon, flailing his arms and legs helplessly, whilst his mother down below fished for him desperately with an umbrella. Shelves were practically toppling over, brimming with things that bounced, squeaked, or billowed smoke. There was no logical layout, no sorting, just pure, noisy pandemonium. My sense of order screamed in agony.

Leo somehow manoeuvred her way through this battlefield and fortunately soon found the telescopes, which were hidden quite absurdly between stacks of stink bombs and fake wands. We paid as quickly as possible. We simply wanted to get out of this madhouse. It had been a truly very, very long and exhausting day. We made just one last quick stop at the butcher's to fetch cat food for Voodoo and Nox. The shop smelled pungently of raw liver and sawdust, but the butcher was absolutely determined to flog us his latest wares: special „Heart-Morsels“ that were supposedly enchanted with a complex loyalty charm. He explained to us with a straight face that these treats would elevate the „magical bond“ between warlock and beast to a spiritual plane.

I really had to bite my tongue hard so as not to laugh at him. Emma, that is truly a groundbreaking invention. Out in the normal world, one simply calls that „positive reinforcement“. I know this because it is exactly what I researched when Voodoo first turned up in our back yard back then. He was quite different from the stray cats we had been warned about. From the very first second he was open, playful, and looked at me with large, curious eyes. I fell in love instantly. But I was terrified he would simply move on and never return.

So I did what I always do when I want to solve a problem: I went to the library. In a book on behavioural research, I came across Ivan Pavlov. The principle is simple: food equals positive association. I saved my pocket money for the best meat scraps, simply to ensure that Voodoo had a reason to keep coming back to me. That is no mystical soul-connection; that is quite simply biology and a full stomach.

But here in this world, they would probably sell you water as a „liquid thirst-quenching potion“ and charge three Galleons for it. It is fascinating how common sense can be packaged as expensive magic.

But you know, Emma, the actual highlight of the day only came right at the very end. When we were finally back at „The Black Tomcat“, utterly shattered by the impressions, we all squeezed into the small bathroom together after supper to get ready for bed.

And suddenly, Klara and Genevieve simply bubbled over. All the tension of the day was gone, and they told us the things we had missed whilst we were in the bookshop and sitting on the stone steps of Madam Malkin's. They were so excited and full of joy that they mumbled whilst speaking because they had their toothbrushes in their mouths, and toothpaste dribbled down their chins because they were laughing so much. For that one moment, in that tiny, peppermint-smelling bathroom, it truly felt as if we were all already friends, and not strangers who had only met the evening before.

Klara began to recount her tale cautiously – I hadn't heard her speak so much at a stretch the entire day. In doing so, her peculiar, yet somehow sweet accent truly struck me for the first time. She was still absolutely thrilled by the Niffler. She raved about how incredibly soft its black fur had looked and how droll its long snout had been. She was genuinely sad she hadn't been able to stroke it before it ran away. At which Genevieve dryly interjected that this was probably for the best, as otherwise Klara would presumably no longer have a wristwatch now.

And then Genevieve delivered the actual punchline of the day. She told us that this thieving girl actually plans to go into Hufflepuff. Not because she is particularly hardworking, mind you, but for purely pragmatic reasons: she wants to learn how to better train magical creatures there, in order to break her Niffler of its constant pilfering.

Leo was standing there with her toothbrush in her mouth when she heard this, and nearly choked on it out of sheer indignation. She coughed up a small cloud of toothpaste froth and flailed her arms wildly. Then – still frothing at the mouth and brush in hand – she delivered a fiery, albeit somewhat muffled, lecture on how loyalty and honesty were the foundational pillars of her house. A thief in Hufflepuff was evidently a personal insult to her; such a thing would simply not occur in her house.

Furthermore, the girl was apparently terrified that someone might sneak on her. For Nifflers are strictly forbidden at Hogwarts – which, given their penchant for anything that glitters and isn't nailed down, is quite understandable. A school full of valuable artefacts and a kleptomaniac burrowing beast do not make for a good combination.

Genevieve, ever the cool pragmatist, had incidentally tried to exploit the situation outside the shop as cold as ice. She told us quite dryly that she had attempted to capitalise on the girl's panic. She not only wanted to extort a formal apology for Leo, but also negotiate a small sum of „hush money“. The plan backfired, however. The girl apparently broke down completely, tearfully swore to give the Niffler away at once, and then fled in a panic before Genevieve could even begin to negotiate. Should Leo actually end up in the same house as that bundle of nerves, the school year will certainly be highly entertaining.

Then they reported on „Sugarplum's Sweetshop“. And Emma, going by their description – which was repeatedly interrupted by hysterical giggling – that shop must have been pure sensory madness. An olfactory nightmare for me, a paradise for Klara. There were apparently all smells and flavours at once, a sticky cacophony. Everything was brightly coloured, loud, and clogged with first-years experiencing acute sugar shock.

Genevieve looked at me in the mirror whilst she was washing her face, and said quite honestly that I should probably have hated the place. It was simply „too much of everything“. She is probably right; my brain would likely have switched off from the noise – even if a tiny, gluttonous part of me should rather like to know what magical chocolate tastes like.

But the best part of their story was Klara. Quiet, reserved Klara had apparently completely taken the helm in there. She simply took Genevieve energetically by the hand, dragged her through the crowd straight to an enormous, magical tree made of pure candyfloss, and stuffed her face as if there were no tomorrow. That explained the sticky face, then. Priorities, Emma.

And then came the bombshell. Perfect, ever-proper Genevieve Boot admitted quite casually, whilst washing her face, that she had „accidentally“ pinched a few sweets in the chaos. We all burst into roaring laughter. The situation was so absurd. Leo had to hold onto the washbasin to keep from falling over, spraying toothpaste froth in all directions as she did so – I believe I even have some in my hair. But it didn't matter. After all the magic, the fears, and the chaos, this moment was simply lovely.

But there was a more serious moment, too. One that showed me there is much more behind Genevieve's cool façade. As the laughter slowly subsided, Klara was the first to finish. Humming quietly to herself, she went on ahead back into our room.

Scarcely had the door clicked shut when Genevieve's expression changed abruptly. The amused grin vanished, and her look became serious, almost a little worried. She stepped a fraction closer to Leo and me, and dropped her voice to a confidential whisper. She told us that Klara had had massive trouble paying again in the bookshop. Genevieve had observed Klara handing the assistant a handful of coins – far too much for the book. And that scoundrel behind the counter hadn't corrected her in the slightest. He simply pocketed the money without a word. He fleeced an eleven-year-old child as cold as ice.

The worst part of it, according to Genevieve, was not the money, however. It was Klara's expression. She had apparently been so proud to have seemingly managed the „correct“ sum all by herself. Genevieve said she hadn't the heart to crush that little triumph in that moment and expose her before the shop assistant and the other customers. That was so kind. Instead, she has now asked us to discreetly keep an eye on Klara's finances as a group in future. Simply so that she isn't constantly taken for a ride, without making her feel incompetent. We ought simply to show a bit of solidarity and look out for her.

You know, Emma, that truly impressed me. It was such a quiet, unagitated sort of loyalty. I looked at myself briefly in the mirror and had to admit to myself: I like them. I truly like them all. Leo with her chaotic passion, Klara with her quiet manner, and now Genevieve, who stands before her friends like a silent guardian. Normally I find people my age exhausting, loud, and superficial. But these three? They are somehow... alright. Perhaps things here will turn out better than I thought after all.

Meanwhile, it has grown completely quiet in the room. The picture has settled down a little. The others are still fast asleep, but the dynamics in the room have altered. Even Leo, who was dreaming so wildly earlier and fighting invisible duels in her sleep, is lying quite relaxed now. The nervous twitching of her hands has ceased, and her breathing is just as deep and steady as Klara's and the still enviably elegant Genevieve's. It seems as though Leo has finally settled her inner battle for today.

The Kneazle at her feet has finally closed his watchful yellow eye, too. He seems to have accepted that no danger emanates from me and my writing, or else he is simply too tired to play the watchdog any longer. Voodoo has meanwhile curled himself up up here on the wide windowsill too; he is warming my thigh and purring quietly in his sleep.

I suppose I ought to do the same and bring this to a close here. It is almost midnight by now, and my hand is genuinely aching from writing. The alarm clock will drag us mercilessly from our beds awfully early again, too. Breakfast is scheduled for eight o'clock, and then things become serious. At eleven o'clock we must be at King's Cross, where this ominous train to Hogwarts departs. I shall probably need every minute of sleep I can get in order to be braced for what comes tomorrow.

I am completely worn out. But somehow, genuinely content, too.

We miss you.

Sleep well.

Yours, Ella

<!-- Die Logik für Balken und Speichern -->
<script>
document.addEventListener("DOMContentLoaded", function() {

  // --- A. Lesezeichen-Logik ---
    const saveBtn = document.getElementById("save-bookmark");
    const loadBtn = document.getElementById("load-bookmark");
    const deleteBtn = document.getElementById("delete-bookmark");

    // Generiert für jedes Kapitel einen eigenen Schlüssel
    const pageKey = "bookmark-" + window.location.pathname;

    // Prüft, ob es für dieses Kapitel schon ein Lesezeichen gibt
    const savedPosition = localStorage.getItem(pageKey);
    if (savedPosition) {
      loadBtn.style.display = "inline-block";
      deleteBtn.style.display = "inline-block";
    }

    // Was passiert beim Speichern
    saveBtn.addEventListener("click", function() {
      localStorage.setItem(pageKey, window.scrollY);
      alert('„Dein Lesezeichen wurde erfolgreich gespeichert!“');
      loadBtn.style.display = "inline-block";
      deleteBtn.style.display = "inline-block";
    });

    // Was passiert beim Laden
    loadBtn.addEventListener("click", function() {
      window.scrollTo({
        top: parseInt(localStorage.getItem(pageKey), 10),
        behavior: "smooth"
      });
    });

    // Was passiert beim Löschen
    deleteBtn.addEventListener("click", function() {
      localStorage.removeItem(pageKey);
      alert('„Das Lesezeichen wurde entfernt.“');
      loadBtn.style.display = "none";
      deleteBtn.style.display = "none";
    });

  // --- B. Fortschrittsbalken-Logik ---
  window.addEventListener('scroll', function() {
    let winScroll = document.body.scrollTop || document.documentElement.scrollTop;
    let height = document.documentElement.scrollHeight - document.documentElement.clientHeight;

    // Berechnet die Prozente
    let scrolled = (winScroll / height) * 100;

    // Verhindert, dass der Balken über den Rand hinauswächst (z.B. bei Handys)
    if (scrolled > 100) scrolled = 100;
    if (scrolled < 0) scrolled = 0;

    // Zeichnet den Balken
    document.getElementById("myBar").style.width = scrolled + "%";
  });
  // --- C. Fortschrittsbalken exakt an die Textbox anpassen ---
  // HIER BITTE DEN KLASSENNAMEN DEINER TEXTBOX EINTRAGEN (z.B. .content oder .story-container)
  const textBox = document.querySelector('.markdown-section');
  const progressContainer = document.querySelector('.progress-container');

  function matchProgressToTextBox() {
    if (textBox && progressContainer) {
      const rect = textBox.getBoundingClientRect();

      // Setzt die Position und Breite des Balkens exakt auf die Werte der Textbox
      progressContainer.style.left = rect.left + 'px';
      progressContainer.style.width = rect.width + 'px';
    }
  }

  // Beim Laden, beim Scrollen und bei Größenänderung des Fensters anpassen
  matchProgressToTextBox();
  window.addEventListener('resize', matchProgressToTextBox);
  window.addEventListener('scroll', matchProgressToTextBox);
});
</script>
